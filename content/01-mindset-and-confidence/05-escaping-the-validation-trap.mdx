## The drug and the trap

Likes feel good. Comments feel good. A client saying "this is amazing" feels good. None of that is the problem.

The problem is when those small hits become the reason you do the work. The moment external validation becomes the engine, the work starts to bend toward whatever is most likely to get a reaction. Quick takes. Trends. Posts that look like the ones already winning the algorithm. The work gets shallower while feeling busier.

This page is about how to spot the trap, and how to swap the engine without becoming a hermit who ignores all feedback.

## What "intrinsic motivation" really means

The phrase sounds like a textbook. The idea is simple.

Intrinsic motivation is when you do the work because the work itself is interesting, useful, or meaningful to you. The reward lives inside the doing. You would still do some version of it if no one was watching.

External validation is the opposite. The reward is what other people give you when you finish. Likes, applause, praise, awards.

Both can exist at the same time. The question is which one is in the driver's seat. If the work itself is the engine, validation is a nice bonus when it shows up. If validation is the engine, the work starts to drift.

> **Definition:** Validation trap. The slow drift that happens when external feedback becomes the main fuel for your work. Trends replace ideas, attention replaces purpose, and over time the value of your work goes down even as you produce more of it.

## How the trap closes around you

The trap closes in a quiet loop.

You make work. Some of it does well online. You notice what does well. Without quite deciding to, you make more of that. Your output becomes more like what people are already rewarding. It is faster to make, easier to copy, and easier to compare to other people doing the same thing. Your prices stop holding because the work no longer looks unique. You work harder for less money. The dopamine from the next post has to do the work that meaning used to do.

The creatives who command real respect and real fees almost never live inside this loop. They make work for a reason that is not "will this get likes." They make it for a client outcome they care about. For a problem they cannot stop thinking about. For a personal idea that has been knocking around for months.

That is not luck. That is what stable motivation produces over time.

## The swap: validation to information

Switching off external feedback entirely is the wrong move. You would lose useful signal. The right move is changing what you are listening to feedback for.

Old question, when feedback comes in: "Am I good enough?"

New question, when feedback comes in: "Did this work? What can I learn from it?"

The first question is about you. The second is about the work.

Because the second question is about the work, it does not bruise. You can read a tough comment and stay curious. You can read a glowing comment and still ask which part landed and why. You stop being a person waiting for a verdict and become a craftsperson reading the room.

> **Tip:** A small move that helps: when feedback arrives, write the comment down separately from your reaction to it. Two columns. The comment in one. What you can use from it in the other. The second column is shorter than you would expect.

## What happens when you take praise the same way

Most people do not realize they have a praise problem until they meet a piece of strong negative feedback. Then they react badly because the praise was secretly carrying them.

Praise can be just as misleading as criticism. People often praise the things that were easiest for you to make. Or the things that look like other work they already liked. Or the things that match their taste, not their problem.

Treat praise like data, not like a verdict on you.

**Example.**

**Client:** "I love it. This is exactly what we needed."

Old reaction: a small wave of relief, end of conversation.

Better reaction: "Thank you. I am curious which part felt the most useful, and which part you would change if you could. I want to understand why this worked so I can do it again."

The client almost always tells you something interesting. You learn what to repeat.

## Finding what energizes you when no one is watching

If you want to know what your real motivators are, look at what you do when there is no audience.

Three questions usually surface them.

1. What parts of your work do you enjoy even when nothing about that work will ever be visible to anyone else?
2. What problems do you find yourself thinking about in the shower, on a walk, or right before sleep, just because they are interesting?
3. What kind of impact, on what kind of people, would make you feel like the work was worth it, even if no one ever clapped for it?

The answers do not have to be noble. Some people light up around clean execution. Some around helping a specific kind of person. Some around an aesthetic problem that has nothing to do with their clients. Honest answers point at where your stable fuel actually lives.

> **Warning:** If your honest answer to all three is "the praise," you have not found a real motivator yet. That is fine. Keep looking. The real one is usually quieter and easier to miss.

## Tracing it back to what already worked

Sometimes the way to find a real motivator is not to look forward but to look back.

Think about the last time creative work felt easy in a good way. Not effortless, but absorbing. You lost track of time. You did not need to be told what to do next. You did not need anyone to tell you it was good.

What were you doing? Who was it for? What part of you was being used?

That memory is a clue about what you should design more of your work around. Most adults can find one or two of these moments, often going back to childhood, where the work itself was the reward. Those moments are not nostalgia. They are evidence.

## Try this

Pick the next piece of public work you are about to ship. Before you publish it, write down two sentences.

The first sentence: "The reason I made this is _____." Fill it in honestly. If the honest answer is "I want this to do well online," write that. Do not pretty it up.

The second sentence: "If this gets very little attention, I will still be glad I made it because _____." Fill that one in too.

If you cannot finish the second sentence, the work is being driven mostly by the validation engine. That is not a reason to scrap it. It is a reason to notice. Over time, more and more of your work should pass the second-sentence test. That is what the swap looks like in practice.

## Summary

Likes are pleasant. As fuel for your work, they are unstable, and the work bends toward whatever the algorithm rewards this month. Intrinsic motivation, doing the work because the work itself is interesting or useful, is a much steadier engine. Do not stop reading feedback. Change what you are reading it for. Listen for information about the work, not for verdicts on you. Use the same lens on praise that you use on criticism. Look at what you do when no one is watching. That is where your real motivators live.
