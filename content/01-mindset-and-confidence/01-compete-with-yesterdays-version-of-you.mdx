## The wrong scoreboard

Most creatives carry a scoreboard in their head. They check it every time they open Instagram, LinkedIn, or someone else's portfolio. The score is built from things they cannot control: somebody else's clients, somebody else's reach, somebody else's life situation.

That scoreboard is a trap. You can play that game your whole life and never feel like you are winning.

This page is the simple swap. You stop comparing yourself to other people. You start comparing yourself only to who you were six months ago. The rules of the game change. Suddenly you can win it.

## Why external comparison is broken

When you compete against another creative, you are playing a game with rules you did not write.

They started earlier. They had a different network. They got lucky on one project. They live in a different city. They define success differently than you do.

You can outwork them and still feel behind, because the variables are not the same. You can also "beat" them and realize you never wanted what they have in the first place.

> **Warning:** External comparison feels like ambition. It is usually just anxiety wearing a suit. Real ambition has a direction. Comparison only has an opponent.

## What internal competition looks like

Internal competition has one rule. The only person you have to beat is the version of you that existed six months ago.

That version had less skill. Fewer reps. A smaller body of work. A worse sense of how to talk to clients. You know that person. You can compete against them fairly because you know exactly what they were like.

When you frame it this way, two things happen. First, you stop wasting energy on comparisons you cannot win. Second, you start noticing real progress, because the benchmark is something you can measure.

**Example.** James Dyson built more than five thousand prototypes of his vacuum cleaner before the one that worked. He did not measure himself against Hoover. He measured himself against the prototype he had built the day before. Each version was a small step past the last one. He competed with yesterday's Dyson, and he won that competition five thousand times in a row.

## How to swap the scoreboard

The swap is mostly about how you phrase the question in your own head.

Old question: "Why did they get that project and I did not?"

New question: "What skill do I need to get projects like that?"

Old question: "Why is their work better than mine?"

New question: "How do I make my next project better than my last one?"

The old questions point outward and have no useful answer. The new questions point inward and produce a to-do list.

> **Tip:** When you catch yourself in the old question, you do not need to fight it. Just rewrite it. The rewrite is the whole exercise.

## The "see, don't compare" move

You do not have to stop looking at other people's work. That is impossible and it would also make you worse at your craft. Looking at great work is how you learn.

What changes is what you do with the feeling that comes up.

When you see something amazing, the lazy reaction is "I will never be that good." The useful reaction is "I see something there I want to try." One reaction shrinks you. The other one expands what you are willing to attempt.

The mental move is small. Replace the feeling of being smaller with curiosity. Curiosity does not hurt and it leaves you with something to do.

**Example.** A designer sees a portfolio piece that is far better than anything they have made. Bad version: "I am wasting my time, I will never get there." Good version: "The way they used type at the bottom of that page is interesting. I want to try something like that on my next project."

## When the inner critic is louder than usual

Some weeks the comparison is louder. You see a peer announce a new client, a book deal, or a speaking gig, and the old scoreboard lights up.

In those weeks, do not argue with the feeling. Just write down the answer to one question.

"What did the version of me from six months ago not know how to do, that I can do now?"

The list is always longer than you expect. That list is the real scoreboard.

## Try this

Open a notes file. Title it "Yesterday's Version of Me."

Write five things. They can be small. A skill you got better at. A type of conversation you used to dread but can now handle. A client situation you would have panicked in six months ago and did not panic in last week. A new tool you use without thinking. A piece of your work that is clearly better than the one before it.

Read the list back. That is the only competition that counts.

Bookmark the file. Add to it once a month. When the external scoreboard lights up, open the file instead.

## Summary

External comparison is a game with rules you did not write and cannot win. Internal competition is a game with one player, you, and one rule, beat last year's version. When you see better work, replace the urge to shrink with curiosity about a single technique. Keep a short, written list of what you can now do that you could not do before. The list is the only scoreboard you need.
