## What this page is about

You have a candidate niche. The three circles overlap. The Five Ones framework looks promising on paper. Now the harder question. Is this thing real, or are you in love with an idea that the market is going to ignore?

Most failed specializations were not killed by competition or bad timing. They were killed by a quiet mismatch the founder never tested. The audience was too small. The buyers did not actually pay. The problem was not as painful as it sounded in conversation. The market existed, but you could not reach it.

This page is about a small ladder of tests that surface those problems before you spend a year discovering them. It is not about being certain. It is about not being foolish.

## Try to kill the idea first

A useful flip. Most people, when they find an idea they like, look for evidence to support it. The opposite move is more useful. Look for evidence that the idea is wrong. If you cannot kill it after a real attempt, that is your first piece of validation.

A few questions sharpen the attempt.

- Who specifically needs this, and what hard evidence do you have?
- What are people doing about this problem today? Why is that not enough?
- What conditions would have to be true for someone to pay you for this?
- How would you actually reach these people? On which channel? Through which network?
- What test could you run in the next two weeks that costs almost nothing and would tell you whether the demand is real?

If you cannot answer these without leaning on hope, the idea is not yet ready to commit to. That is not a failure. It is information. Get the information before you get the bill.

> **Warning:** "Friends were excited" is not validation. People who like you will support most of your ideas. The signal you want is from people who do not know you, will not feel awkward saying no, and have a checkbook on their desk.

## The five levels of validation

There is a useful ladder of evidence that gets stronger as you climb. Each level tells you something the previous one cannot.

**Level 1. Conversation validation.** Find at least five people in your target market who immediately understand the problem when you describe it. Not "oh, I see, that is interesting." Real recognition. They start finishing your sentences. They tell you a story about their own version of it. If you have to keep explaining the problem, the problem is not pulling at them strongly enough yet.

**Level 2. Payment validation.** Will anyone commit money before the thing exists in finished form? A deposit on a future project. A pre-sold workshop. A small package they pay for now and you deliver in three weeks. Money commitments are stronger signals than verbal enthusiasm by a long way. People say nice things at coffee. They say true things with their wallet.

**Level 3. Usage validation.** Once you have delivered something, do they actually use it? An advisor people pay but never call back to is not really being paid for advice. They paid for relief from the decision to look. Usage tells you whether what you are selling is what they wanted.

**Level 4. Outcome validation.** Does it actually solve the problem? Track real results. Revenue change. Lead quality. Time saved. Process unblocked. If you cannot point to outcomes, you have a fee, not a value proposition.

**Level 5. Referral validation.** Do happy clients send other people to you, unprompted? Referrals are the strongest signal because the referrer is putting their reputation on the line. People do not refer mediocre work, even from friends. If your clients refer, the loop is closed.

The ladder is not optional. If you skip levels and assume, you will miss problems that only become visible at the next step.

> **Definition:** Validation. Evidence from outside your own head that the idea is working. Conversations and payments are external. Excitement and enthusiasm are not.

## What level you are stuck on tells you something specific

The level you cannot get past is usually a clue about which part of the niche is weak.

- **Stuck at conversation.** The problem statement is wrong, or the audience does not actually have this problem in the form you are describing. Reframe before you keep building.
- **Stuck at payment.** The problem is real, but the urgency or the priority is too low to justify spending. Find a sharper version of the problem, or a more painful moment in the buyer's calendar.
- **Stuck at usage.** Your offer is treating a symptom, not the underlying issue. People are willing to pay for relief but the relief is not landing where the pain is.
- **Stuck at outcome.** The intervention is too small, the timeline is too short, or the metric is wrong. You may also be doing the right thing for the wrong client.
- **Stuck at referral.** The work is good but not memorable. Often this is a story problem, not a craft problem. Clients do not have the language to describe what you did, so they cannot recommend you.

Most specializations die at one specific level. Knowing which one tells you what to fix.

## Cheap, fast experiments beat expensive, slow ones

A common trap. People design one big test. They build a full offer. They invest months. The test "fails" and they conclude the niche does not work, when in fact the test was simply too expensive to run honestly.

Better pattern. Run small tests, often, that take days, not months.

A few that work.

- A short landing page describing the offer, plus a paid ad campaign with a small budget. Do people click? Do they fill in the form?
- A pre-sold version of the work at a discount, before any of it is built. Will five people commit money based on a description?
- A free phone call, but with a specific topic narrow enough to be meaningful. How many real buyers actually book?
- A guest post or a podcast appearance pitched to your exact target audience. Did anyone reach out afterward?

Each of these costs almost nothing and tells you something the abstract conversation cannot.

**Example.** A copywriter is considering specializing in medical-device marketing copy. Instead of building a full site, she writes one detailed teardown of an existing device's website, posts it in a community where medical-device marketers hang out, and waits a week. Three of them message her privately to ask if she takes on freelance work. That cost her one weekend. She has level 1 validation. She has the start of level 2 because two of those three are willing to put a deposit on a small project. She did not need a quarter to learn what a Saturday told her.

> **Tip:** When designing a test, ask: "What is the smallest version of this that would still produce real evidence?" The smaller the test, the more honest the evidence, because you have less ego invested in it.

## Validation never ends

A subtle point worth saying clearly. Validation is not a phase you complete. It is an ongoing practice. Markets shift. Buyer language changes. The problem that was urgent two years ago is solved today. A specialization that worked at year one needs to be re-checked at year three.

The same five levels apply later. Are new conversations still showing recognition? Are new buyers still willing to pay? Are usage and outcomes still where you want them? Are referrals still flowing?

A short annual review handles this. Once a year, walk through the ladder and check the rungs. If one is starting to wobble, you have time to fix it. If you skip the review, you find out at the worst possible moment.

## Try This

Pick your candidate specialization and spend thirty minutes:

1. Write down which level of validation you are honestly at right now. Do not round up.
2. Identify the smallest experiment you can run this week to test the next level.
3. Run it. Note the result, including a literal count of conversations, sign-ups, deposits, or replies.
4. Compare what you expected to what happened. The gap is the lesson.

If you find yourself reluctant to actually run the test, that reluctance is information too. Usually it means part of you already knows what the test will say.

## Summary

A specialization that has not been tested is just an opinion in nicer clothing. The validation ladder gives you five levels of evidence, each stronger than the last: conversation, payment, usage, outcome, and referral. The level you cannot pass tells you exactly which part of your niche is weak. Cheap, fast experiments beat expensive, slow ones because they protect honesty. Validation does not end after launch; the same checks belong in your yearly review. Trying to kill your idea first is a feature, not a flaw. The faster you find the holes, the less time you spend filling the wrong ones.
