## The multi-trunk problem

Talented people often have a brand problem that talented people uniquely create.

They are good at three things. They are interested in five. They have opinions about ten. So they post about all of them. They write about pricing on Monday, design critique on Tuesday, leadership on Wednesday, productivity on Thursday, and a personal essay on Friday. Each post is fine. The body of work, taken together, is unfocused.

The result is not a powerful, multi-faceted brand. It is three or four weak brands sharing a single account. People who land on the work cannot quickly tell what this person is for. They follow vaguely or move on.

This page is the right shape to hold many interests inside one coherent brand. The model that works best is a tree. One trunk. A few branches. Eventually, treehouses on top.

## The tree model

Think of your brand as a tree.

The trunk is the one thing you most want to be known for. It should be deep and stable. Most of your visible work should reinforce it. People should be able to point at the trunk in one short sentence and say "that is what this person does."

The branches are related interests and capabilities that grow out of the trunk. They support the trunk. They feed it energy. They are visible, but they are clearly part of the same tree.

The treehouses are new structures you can add later, after the trunk is strong. New topics, new content areas, new ventures that rest on the established authority of the trunk. They are only viable once the underlying tree can hold the weight.

The mistake most people make is trying to grow several main trunks at once. A multi-trunk tree falls in a single strong wind. A single deep trunk with many branches is sturdy.

> **Definition:** Tree model of personal brand. The architecture in which one core focus (the trunk) is established first, related interests grow as branches, and new dimensions are added later as treehouses that rest on the established trunk.

## Build the trunk first

The trunk is what you most want to be known for. Choose it on purpose.

A good trunk has four characteristics. It is specific enough that someone can describe it in a sentence. It is something you find genuinely interesting, because you will spend years on it. It produces real value for a real audience. And there is enough material in it to sustain a body of work over time.

Start by writing the trunk down in one sentence. "I help small SaaS teams turn confusing products into clear ones." "I write about how creative work gets priced." "I teach engineers how to communicate with non-technical stakeholders." A sentence like that is a trunk.

Then, for the next year or two, do most of your visible work in service of that sentence. Most. Not all. The branches are coming. But the proportions matter.

> **Tip:** A useful split early on is roughly seventy percent of visible output on the trunk, twenty percent on related branches, and ten percent on whatever else is interesting. The percentages do not have to be exact. The hierarchy has to be clear.

## Branches feed the trunk

Once the trunk is starting to take hold, you can let branches grow.

Branches are related topics and capabilities that connect back to the trunk in a way the audience can see. If your trunk is "pricing for creative work," related branches might be value-based selling, client communication, or how creative careers grow. They are not the trunk, but they are clearly nearby.

Branches do two things. They give your audience more reasons to stay engaged, because not every post is the same exact topic. And they feed back into the trunk, because the related work makes you smarter and more credible on the core thing.

The danger with branches is letting any one of them quietly grow into a second trunk. If you find yourself spending half your output on a "branch," it is no longer a branch. It is a competing trunk. At that point, you either decide to retire the original trunk and let the new one take over, or you prune the branch back. What you cannot do without confusing the audience is keep two trunks running.

## Treehouses come last

Treehouses are entirely new structures you build on top of an established tree.

A new course. A new niche. A book about a topic that is not exactly your trunk. A podcast about something adjacent. A consulting offer in a different domain. These are treehouses. They can carry serious weight, but only if the tree underneath them can support it.

The order is important. You cannot start with a treehouse. If you talk about a new topic on day one, before any trunk exists, you are just a person talking. There is no underlying authority to lend the new topic credibility. People do not yet have a reason to take you seriously on it.

Once the tree is established, the same is not true. People who trust you on the trunk will give you a hearing on a new area, because the trust transfers from you-the-person to whatever you say next. That is when adding new dimensions becomes powerful.

The sequence is non-negotiable for most people. Trunk first. Branches second. Treehouses last.

> **Warning:** Skipping straight to a treehouse is one of the most common reasons new ventures fail to gain traction. Without a trunk underneath, the new project has to earn attention from scratch, on its own merits, against everyone else doing the same thing.

## A useful side effect: portable trust

There is a related advantage to building this way that is not obvious at first.

If your audience followed you because of you, not just because of a topic, your trust is portable. You can change directions, take on new kinds of projects, or move into adjacent industries, and a meaningful piece of your audience will come with you.

This is qualitatively different from a topic-only following. A subject-matter expert whose entire identity is "the Excel person" cannot easily move into a different domain, because the audience came for the topic. A personal brand whose audience came for the person can move, because the relationship was attached to the person, not the subject.

This portability is one of the highest-value outcomes of building a personal brand the right way. It is also another reason to invest in the trunk for longer than feels necessary. The deeper the trust in the trunk, the more it can travel later.

## Narrowing is not shrinking

Most people resist the idea of having a single trunk because they worry it will shrink their reach.

It does the opposite. A focused trunk reaches fewer people but moves them more. A scattered brand reaches more people and moves none of them.

A useful image. Imagine a transmitter. If you broadcast on a wide, fuzzy frequency, more devices can pick up some signal, but none of them get a clear one. If you broadcast on a narrow, specific frequency, only the devices tuned to that exact frequency hear you, but they hear you clearly. The narrow signal produces deep alignment with a small group. The wide signal produces shallow attention from a larger group.

Personal brand works the same way. The people who tune in to a narrow, specific signal feel like they finally found something made for them. That feeling produces loyalty, referrals, and repeat work. A general signal does not produce that, no matter how large the audience.

This is also why the question "but what if I narrow my focus and lose followers?" is usually the wrong question. The followers you lose were not aligned in the first place. The ones you keep, and the ones you gain, are the ones who produce real outcomes.

> **Tip:** When narrowing feels scary, remember that your real audience is rooting for you to be more fully yourself. They do not want a diluted version that tries to please everyone. They want the clear version.

## Try this

Take a sheet of paper. Draw a tree. Big trunk in the middle. Several branches.

Write your trunk in one sentence on the trunk itself. If you cannot do this in one sentence, the trunk is not yet defined and that is the most important work you have. Try again until one sentence holds.

On each branch, write a related interest, capability, or content area. Three to five branches is plenty. Each branch should clearly connect to the trunk. If a branch does not connect, it is a different tree.

Look at your last month of public output. Estimate how much of it was trunk, how much was branch, how much was unrelated. If the unrelated category is larger than ten or fifteen percent, that is the source of the unfocused feeling people get when they encounter your work. Pull it back for the next quarter and watch what happens.

## Summary

Most multi-talented people accidentally build several weak brands instead of one strong one. The fix is the tree model. One trunk, deep and stable. A few branches that connect to and feed the trunk. Treehouses, new dimensions and ventures, only after the underlying tree can hold them. The order is non-negotiable. Building this way produces portable trust, the kind that follows you across changes in topic, industry, or format. Narrowing your focus does not shrink your impact. It concentrates it. The audience aligned to a clear signal is more valuable than a larger audience attached to a fuzzy one.
