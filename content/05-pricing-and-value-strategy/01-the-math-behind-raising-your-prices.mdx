## Why this is the post you keep avoiding

Most people who want to charge more never do. Not because the market said no. Because they never asked.

The story we tell ourselves is that if we raise our prices, the work will dry up. Clients will leave. Pipeline will collapse. We will sit in front of an empty inbox and wonder why we did this to ourselves.

That story is almost never true. The math, in fact, says the opposite. This page walks through it slowly, with real numbers, so you can run it on your own business after you read.

## The simple version

Picture two versions of you.

Old you charges 2,500 dollars per project. Ten clients a year. That is 25,000 dollars in revenue.

New you charges 20,000 dollars per project. Same year, same effort. To match old you, new you only needs **two** clients. To beat old you, new you needs three.

Read that again. The new price is eight times higher. The number of clients you need is **five times lower**. Even if it takes longer to find each one, you have a lot of room.

| Version | Price per project | Clients needed to match | Clients needed to beat |
|----|----|----|----|
| Old you | 2,500 | 10 | 11 |
| New you | 20,000 | 2 | 3 |

This is the math people refuse to do before they decide raising prices is too risky. It is not an opinion. It is arithmetic.

## Why higher prices take longer to close

A higher price tag changes the kind of conversation you are in. That is not a bug. It is what a bigger sale is supposed to feel like.

Think about your own buying. When you grab a pack of gum at the register, you do not consult your spouse. When you buy a fridge, you read reviews, compare brands, check the dimensions, talk it over, sleep on it. The gum decision takes seconds. The fridge decision takes weeks.

Your services work the same way. A 2,500 dollar project is a gum purchase. A 20,000 dollar project is a fridge. Of course it takes longer. It should.

The mistake is to read "this is taking longer" as "this is not working". It is working. The cycle is just on a different clock.

> **Tip:** Track the time from first contact to signed contract for your last five projects. If you raise your price four times, expect that number to grow. Plan for it on paper before it shows up in your calendar and freaks you out.

## You have to become a different kind of business

Here is the part nobody warns you about. The reason new clients do not just hand you eight times more money is that, today, you are not yet the person they would hand it to.

The site, the language, the offer, the case studies, the photos, the way you talk on a call. All of that was built for the 2,500 dollar version of your business. A 20,000 dollar buyer scans it and does not see themselves in it. They scroll on.

So the work is not just to raise the number on the proposal. The work is to look like, sound like, and behave like a business that charges that number.

That includes:

- Positioning in your headline. What you sell, and to whom.
- Case studies in language and numbers your buyer cares about.
- The offer itself. Scope, timeline, what is in and what is out.
- The way you reply to the first email. Calm, clear, not desperate.
- The price on the proposal. No discount, no apology.

If you raise the price without changing any of the rest, you are just the cheap version of you with a bigger number on it. That is not the same thing.

## The "second site" trick

Sometimes you cannot, or do not want to, kill the old version of your business while you build the new one. The old work is paying the bills. The new work is a bet.

A simple move: build a second presence. New site. New name for the offer. New positioning. New language. New photos. New case studies, even if it is only one or two at the start.

When you go after 20,000 dollar clients, you point them at the new site. The old site keeps doing its job for the smaller projects. You are not lying. You are building two doors into different rooms.

Over time, the new room fills up and the old one empties. That is the migration. It does not happen on a Tuesday. It happens over a year.

> **Warning:** If both rooms are open and you keep accepting smaller projects out of fear, the bigger projects never close. The bandwidth in your head is finite. At some point you have to choose where you actually want to play.

## What gets in the way is not the market

The biggest reason people do not raise prices is not pipeline. It is identity.

There is a quote, often attributed to Jim Rohn, that lives at the bottom of this whole thing: the goal is not what you get, it is who you have to become to get it. That is the real work behind a price increase. You have to become the person whose work, whose calm, whose clarity is worth that number. The market will follow. It almost always does.

So the question is not "can I afford to raise my prices". It is "am I willing to do the work of becoming the version of me who charges this".

If the answer is yes, the math is on your side. The math has been on your side the whole time.

## Try This

Pull out a calculator. Spend ten minutes on this:

1. Write down your current average project price. Call it A.
2. Write the price you actually want to charge. Call it B.
3. Divide B by A. That is how much fewer clients you need at the new price to match your current revenue.
4. Look at the number. Is it scary, or is it freeing?
5. Now write one sentence that describes the kind of buyer who pays B. Specific. Role, situation, outcome they want.

If you cannot describe that buyer in one sentence, your real problem is not pricing. It is positioning. Go back to **01. Finding the Clients You Actually Want** and do that work first.

## Summary

The fear of raising prices is almost always bigger than the math. A higher price means fewer clients, longer sales cycles, and a different kind of business. None of that is a problem. It is the design. The shift is mostly internal. You become the person who charges the new number, the rest of the system catches up, and one day the old prices look strange to you. The sooner you do the math on paper, the sooner the fear stops running your pipeline.
