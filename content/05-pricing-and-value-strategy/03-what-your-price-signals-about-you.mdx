## The number is also a story

Two designers sit side by side at the same conference. They do similar work. One quotes 2,500 dollars for a brand identity. The other quotes 25,000.

The buyer is not just choosing between two prices. The buyer is reading two different stories. The 2,500 dollar quote tells one kind of story about the work, the process, and the risk involved. The 25,000 dollar quote tells a different one. The numbers carry weight beyond what they buy.

This page is about the story your price tells before you say a single word about your process.

## Clients do not buy the most creative option. They buy the safest one.

This part is hard to swallow because it goes against what we like to believe. Most clients are not looking for the most original or the most beautiful work. They are looking for the option least likely to blow up in their face.

A new client hiring a freelancer is taking a risk. Their boss is watching. The budget came from somewhere. If the project fails, somebody on their side has to explain it. The buyer's silent question, while you talk, is: "Will this person make me look smart for hiring them?"

Price is one of the strongest signals about that risk. A surprisingly low price reads as desperation. The buyer thinks: this person needs the work, so they cut corners on the price, so maybe they will cut corners on the work too. A confident, normal-for-the-tier price reads as proven. The buyer thinks: this person has done this before, and they know what it is worth.

A premium price is a reassurance, not a hurdle. It is the work telling the buyer: I have done this many times, the process is mine, and the result is not a guess.

> **Tip:** When you think a low price will help you win, run the test the other direction. Imagine the buyer in their boss's office one month from now defending the choice. Which price is easier to defend? The low one rarely is.

## Like attracts like

Pricing pulls clients into your orbit. It also pushes some of them out. Both of those are good, as long as you are intentional about it.

Cheap pricing attracts clients who think your work is a cost. They will haggle. They will push scope. They will treat every revision as a free favor. They are not bad people. The price told them this was a transaction, and they treat it like one.

Premium pricing attracts a different mix. Some buyers walk away because they cannot afford it, or because they do not see the value yet. The ones who stay are usually one of two types. The first care about results and have money to spend on results. The second do not have unlimited money, but care enough about quality to allocate the budget anyway. Both types make better clients. They argue less. They listen more. They do their homework.

This is what people mean by "like attracts like." The number on your proposal sets the room. People who fit walk in. People who do not fit walk out before the meeting starts. That is not snobbery. That is a filter doing its job.

## Every touchpoint has to match

Here is where things break for most people. You raise your price. The website still looks like it did when you charged a fifth of that. The proposal looks the same. The reply email is rushed. The portfolio is a grid of 100 thumbnails.

A 20,000 dollar buyer scans all of that and feels a mismatch. Not consciously. They just feel something is off. They scroll on. The price was right. Everything around it was wrong.

If you want to sell at a higher tier, every contact point has to carry the same weight as the number on the proposal:

- The site. Calm, specific, focused on the kind of buyer you want.
- The portfolio. Fewer pieces, more depth. Outcome stories, not aesthetics.
- The reply email. Same day, professional, no apology, no rambling.
- The proposal. Clean, structured, no typos, no emoji, no enthusiasm-as-substitute-for-clarity.
- The first call. You arrive on time, you ask before you pitch, you do not over-explain.

A way to think about it: imagine your buyer's other vendors. The lawyer. The CPA. The agency they last hired for 50,000 dollars. Your touchpoints should not feel embarrassing next to those.

> **Warning:** A premium price next to a portfolio of 100 thumbnails actually hurts you. The mismatch makes the buyer suspicious. They see the high number, look around for the credibility to back it up, and find a generic grid. Better to show three deep case studies than one hundred thumbnails.

## A small example you can run today

A short test. Open your own site. Pretend you are a buyer with a real budget, looking at it for the first time. Read the headline. Look at the first three things you see. Read the first paragraph of your most recent case study.

Ask one question: "Would I pay 20,000 dollars to the person behind this site?"

Be honest. Most of the time, the answer is "not yet." That is fine. It tells you what to fix first. The price you want to charge has to be supported by what the client sees around the price.

The thing nobody else will tell you is that this work is mostly invisible. Nobody hands you a checklist. The buyer never says "your portfolio felt thin." They just go quiet. The job is to fix what makes them go quiet before you ever get on the call.

## Try This

Spend ten minutes:

1. Write down the price you want to be charging in twelve months.
2. Open your homepage, your most recent case study, and your last reply email to a client.
3. For each one, ask: does this look and sound like it is from a person who charges that number?
4. Mark each item as "yes," "close," or "no." Do not soften the answers.
5. Pick the worst-rated item. That is the first thing you fix this week.

If everything you make today reads "small project," you cannot expect a 20,000 dollar client to recognize themselves in it.

## Summary

Your price is a signal that travels faster than your portfolio. It tells the buyer what kind of risk you are, what kind of process you run, and what kind of client you are used to. Cheap reads as risky. Premium reads as proven, but only if everything else around the price holds the same line. The job is not just to write a bigger number on the proposal. It is to make the rest of the experience match what the number is claiming.
