## What this page is about

You quote a price. There is a pause. The client says one of a few familiar things.

"That is more than we were thinking."
"We need to think about it."
"Can you do it for a bit less?"
"We are getting other quotes."

These are not signs the deal is dead. They are the most predictable moments in a sales conversation. The problem is that most of us have not practiced what to say in them, so we cave. We discount. We shrink the scope. We apologize. The deal closes at a price we resent.

This page is a small playbook for those moments. The goal is not to be slick. It is to stay calm, stay honest, and stay at the price you quoted.

By the end you will be able to:

- Defuse "too expensive" with three different responses, picking the right one for the situation.
- Use the client's own premium choices to reframe what they are buying.
- Name the fear that lives behind the price moment, before the client does.

## Embrace and pivot, the price version

You already know Embrace and Pivot from **01. From Order-Taker to Advisor**, where it works for the very first conversation. The same shape works for price objections, with a different second beat.

The structure has three steps.

1. **Embrace.** Agree that the price is high. "You are right, it is a real investment."
2. **Pivot.** Tell a short story that puts the price in the right frame. "I had a client who said exactly the same thing. Six months later, they told me it was the cheapest decision they had made all year, because [specific result]."
3. **Qualify.** Hand the question back. "Can you help me understand what you were expecting to invest?"

The reason this works is that it does not argue. Arguing puts you on opposite sides. Embracing puts you on the same side, looking at the same problem. The story moves the conversation from price to value. The qualifying question turns a vague objection into a real number you can work with.

> **Tip:** Embracing is not agreeing the price is wrong. It is agreeing that it feels big. Those are different things. Most people lose this in delivery. Practice the embrace until it sounds calm, not defensive.

## The BMW conversation

This is a small move with a long history. The shape is simple. You hold up a mirror to the buyer's own decisions about premium things in their life, then you bridge to the project.

The structure has four steps.

1. **Setup.** Notice something about the buyer that points to a premium choice they have already made. The car. The watch. The office. Their phone. Where they live. The restaurant they suggested. Anything they chose to pay more for.
2. **Open.** "I noticed you drive a [BMW / nice car]. All cars get you from A to B. Why did you choose this one over a basic one?"
3. **Listen.** Let them explain. Take notes. Do not interrupt. Their answer will be about quality, reliability, status, peace of mind, the way it makes them feel. Let them say it in their own words.
4. **Bridge.** "That same logic applies here. We could do something that just gets the job done. Based on the way you think about your other choices, I suspect you actually want something that [reuses their reasoning]."

The point is not to flatter. The point is to remind the buyer they already understand premium. They already pay for it in other parts of their life. They already have a story about why. Borrow their story.

> **Warning:** This has to sound like a real conversation. If it sounds like a script, it lands as manipulation. Practice it on small premium choices first, in low-stakes settings, until it is fluid.

## The accusation audit

This one is sharper. It is for the moment right before you say the price. The fear behind that moment is always the same: that the client will think you are overcharging, taking advantage, or out of touch.

Most people try to bury the fear. They rush past the price. They explain too much. They speak quickly, with an upward inflection at the end of the number, as if asking permission. The buyer hears the hesitation. The hesitation is what loses the deal, not the number.

The accusation audit flips this. You name the fear out loud, then state the number, then stop talking.

A short script.

**You:** "I want to be straightforward with you. I am about to give you a number that might sound higher than what you had in mind. The investment for this project is 18,000 dollars."

Then silence. You do not fill it.

Two things happen. First, naming the fear takes the air out of it. The buyer does not have to be the one to say "that sounds high," because you already said it. Second, the silence forces them to respond with content, not with their first emotional reaction.

This is a Chris Voss move, taken from negotiation work. It works because most of the time, the buyer's response will be milder than the version you were imagining in your head.

## The three ways out of "too expensive"

When a client says some version of "too expensive," there are three sensible paths. Pick the one that fits the situation.

**Path 1: Embrace, pivot, qualify.** Default move. Best when you sense the client wants the work but is checking for room. Walks them through value and asks for their real budget number.

**Path 2: BMW conversation.** Best when you have visible evidence of premium choices in their life or business. Reframes by reminding them how they make other premium decisions.

**Path 3: Strategic no, with a referral.** Best when the gap is genuinely large and there is no honest way to close it without doing damaged work.

A short script for path 3.

**You:** "I appreciate you being open about the budget. To deliver this at the level we have been discussing, the investment needs to be in the range I quoted. Anything less and I would be cutting corners on the part that actually drives the result. I would rather not start that way. Let me put you in touch with [colleague]. They do good work in this range and might be a better fit."

Saying no is uncomfortable. It also keeps your pricing intact, builds goodwill, and often comes back as a referral six months later when the client's budget grows.

> **Warning:** Do not invent a "discount tier" on the fly to save the deal. The discount tier becomes your default. The 18,000 dollar fee never returns. Either hold the line or refer the client out.

## "We need to think about it"

This is rarely about thinking. Almost always, there is a specific concern hiding behind it. Your job is to surface it.

**You:** "Of course. Just so we use the time well, what specifically do you need to think about? Is it the budget, the timing, the scope, or who else needs to weigh in?"

Four options. They will pick one. Now you are talking about the actual blocker, not the fog.

If they say "all of them," that is a buying-not-ready signal, not a no. Ask when a follow-up makes sense, get a date, end the call cleanly.

## "Can you do it for less?"

The honest answer is: yes, but with less work, not at a lower rate.

**You:** "I can absolutely shape something for that budget. Let's not change the price. Let's change the scope. What if we did [smaller version that still delivers something real], and saved [the rest] for a phase two?"

This protects two things. The rate stays where it should. The buyer still feels like they got room to breathe. Most of the time, they take the smaller scope and ask for the rest a few months later anyway.

## Try This

Pick the price-objection situation that has cost you the most money in the last year. Spend twenty minutes:

1. Write down the exact words the client used.
2. For each of the three paths above, write what you would say next time. Use plain language. No script-y phrases.
3. Read them out loud. Mark the one that sounds least forced.
4. Practice that one twice in front of a mirror or with a friend.
5. Use it on the next call. Do not change strategy mid-call. See how it lands.

You will not get it perfect on the first try. You will get less rattled, faster, and that is the whole point.

## Summary

Price objections are not a sign the deal is broken. They are the moment the deal becomes real. Embrace and pivot puts you on the client's side. The BMW conversation borrows the client's own logic about premium choices. The accusation audit names the fear before the client can. "Too expensive" has three honest answers, including a clean no. None of these are tricks. They are small habits that let you stay at the price you quoted without losing the room.
