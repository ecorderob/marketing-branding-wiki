## The trap of selling time

A client asks how much you charge. You tell them an hourly rate. They start doing math in their head. They look at the clock. They see you as a meter that ticks while they wait.

That is the world hourly billing puts you in. The faster you get, the less you earn. The better your tools, the less you earn. The smarter your shortcut, the less you earn. The whole system punishes you for being good.

This page is about a simple swap. Stop selling hours. Start selling outcomes. The hours are still there. You just stop putting a price tag on them.

By the end you will be able to:

- See why hourly billing is broken for expert work.
- Translate your service into a business outcome a client can put a number on.
- Write one sentence that anchors your price to that outcome.

## Hourly billing is honest, but it is the wrong kind of honest

Hourly billing feels fair. You worked four hours, you charge for four hours. Nobody can argue. That is exactly why it is the wrong tool.

A pediatric surgeon does not charge by the hour. The fee is for fixing the heart. If the surgery takes two hours instead of six because the surgeon is good, nobody asks for a discount. The price is tied to the outcome, not the clock.

Creative and consulting work is closer to surgery than to manual labor. The value is in the judgment, not the minutes. When you bill by the hour, you tell the buyer your value is the minutes. They will then negotiate the minutes. That is the conversation you do not want to be in.

> **Definition:** Value-based pricing. A way of pricing where the number on your invoice is tied to the result your work creates for the client, not the hours you spent making it.

## Find the dollar number behind the work

Every job a client hires you for has a real business reason behind it. A logo is rarely about the logo. A website is rarely about the website. A brand strategy is rarely about the brand strategy.

Behind each one, there is a number. Revenue gained. Cost saved. Risk avoided. Deal closed. Investor convinced. Hire attracted.

Your job, before quoting, is to find that number with the client. Not invent it. Find it.

**Example.** A small SaaS founder asks you to redo their landing page. The instinct is to quote 3,000 dollars and start designing.

Better path. You ask: "What happens on this page today, and what would happen if it worked?"

She tells you the page gets 4,000 visitors a month. About 1% sign up for a trial. Each trial that converts to paid is worth 1,200 dollars a year. If conversion went from 1% to 2%, that is 40 extra trials per month, maybe 8 of them paying. Over a year, that is roughly 96 new paying customers, worth around 115,000 dollars in revenue.

Now your 3,000 dollar quote looks strange. Not too high. Too low. The work is worth more because the outcome is worth more. The number to write on your proposal is somewhere between 8,000 and 20,000, depending on how much of the lift you can credibly own.

You did not raise the price by being greedy. You raised it by changing what you were pricing.

> **Tip:** If the client cannot put a dollar figure on the outcome, the project is not ready to be priced. Help them put a number on it. If they refuse to even try, that is a signal: they may not be the buyer for value-based work yet.

## You are not selling the thing. You are selling the change.

Most creative work has a deliverable. A site. A logo. A campaign. A deck. The mistake is to sell the deliverable. The deliverable is the receipt. The product is the change in the client's business.

A useful sentence to keep in your head: "I am not selling a website. I am selling a 24/7 lead-generation asset for the sales team."

Notice what changes when you talk that way. The conversation moves from the design tool to the sales pipeline. From your hours to their revenue. From a cost line in their budget to an investment line.

This is also where Chris Do is sharp. He calls it dollarizing the value. Translate the work into the dollar figure it produces or saves. Once the value has a dollar sign on it, your price stops sounding big. It sounds proportional.

> **Warning:** Do not pitch outcomes you cannot influence. If the client's bigger problem is a broken sales team and you are doing a logo, the logo will not fix the pipeline. Match the outcome you sell to the outcome your work can actually move.

## A simple way to anchor the value before you talk price

Before you say a number, anchor the value. One short script does most of the work.

**You:** "Before we talk about investment, let me make sure I understand what this is worth to you. You said the goal is [their goal]. If we got there, what would that be worth to the business over a year?"

Then listen. Take notes. Repeat their number back. "So we are talking about something in the range of [their number] per year."

Now your price has a frame. A 15,000 dollar fee on a 200,000 dollar problem is a 7% solution. Most clients would happily pay 7% of the problem to make it go away. A 15,000 dollar fee on a 5,000 dollar problem is a no, and it should be. The frame tells you both.

The script is not a trick. It is a way to put both of you in the same conversation, looking at the same number, before any pricing happens.

## Try This

Pick one service you sell today. Spend fifteen minutes:

1. Write down what you currently call it. Probably the deliverable. Logo, site, brand, deck.
2. Write down what the client actually gets from it in their business. One concrete outcome.
3. Put a yearly dollar figure on that outcome. Use the client's own numbers if you have them. Estimate if you do not.
4. Rewrite your one-line pitch as: "I help [type of client] achieve [outcome] worth around [number] per year."
5. Read it out loud. If it sounds bigger than your old pitch, that is the point.

If you cannot put a dollar figure on the outcome, your real job before pricing is to learn to ask the questions that surface it. The first call playbook in **08. The First Call Playbook** is one place to practice.

## Summary

Hourly billing punishes you for being good and pulls the conversation toward minutes instead of value. Value-based pricing flips it. You find the dollar number behind the work, sell the change in the client's business, and let your fee sit as a small percentage of a bigger outcome. The deliverable is the receipt. The outcome is the product. The client buys the outcome.
