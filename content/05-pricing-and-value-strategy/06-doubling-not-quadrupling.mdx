## The two ways people raise prices

When people decide they are underpriced, they tend to make one of two mistakes.

The first one is to never actually do it. They quietly raise prices on the next quote by 10%, the client says yes, and nothing about how the business works changes. A year later, they are still complaining about being underpaid.

The second one is to raise prices too far, too fast. They go from 5,000 dollar projects to 20,000 dollar projects overnight. The pipeline that was full of small jobs goes silent. Two months in, panic sets in. They drop the price back, often below where they started, just to get cash flowing again.

Both mistakes share a cause. The number on the proposal is treated as the only thing that matters. It is not. The number is just one part of a system that has to move together.

This page is about the middle path. Doubling, not quadrupling. Raising in steps the market can absorb and you can actually defend.

## Why doubling works and quadrupling does not

A 2x raise feels big from the inside. It rarely feels big from the outside. The client sees a higher number and a slightly upgraded version of you. They can absorb it. The buyer who was paying 5,000 can usually justify 10,000 with a little extra reassurance. The work feels in the same neighborhood.

A 4x raise crosses neighborhoods. The 5,000 dollar buyer cannot stretch to 20,000 even if they wanted to. To sell at 20,000, you need a different buyer altogether. That buyer does not know you yet. They have not seen your work. They have no reason to trust your price. The leap is not just financial. It is identity.

So the rhythm becomes:

- 5,000 average to 10,000 average.
- 10,000 average to 20,000 average.
- 20,000 average to 40,000 average.

Each step takes time. Three to nine months is normal. During each step, three things move together. Your positioning sharpens. Your case studies deepen. Your confidence in saying the new number out loud catches up with the number itself.

> **Tip:** Do not raise the price on existing clients with active retainers. Raise prices on the next new project. The old clients keep their grandfathered rate for a defined window, then either renew at the new rate or roll off. This avoids the awkward middle conversation and keeps your reputation clean.

## Confidence is preparation, not personality

People talk about pricing confidence as if it is something you have or do not have. That is not how it actually works. What looks like confidence in pricing conversations is usually four boring things stacked together.

1. **Documented objection responses.** You have heard the question before. You have written down what works. You can say it without fumbling.
2. **Case studies with real numbers.** When the client asks "is it worth it," you have an answer. Not a feeling. A number.
3. **Knowing your business numbers.** You know your real costs, your true rate, the floor below which the math breaks. You quote from a position of clarity.
4. **Repetition.** You have said the new number out loud, in real conversations, enough times that it stops sounding strange to your own ear.

If you are missing any of these, the price you want to charge will keep coming out as a question instead of a statement. The fix is not a pep talk. The fix is to build the missing piece.

> **Warning:** Do not raise your price before your case studies and your offer are upgraded. The buyer reads them as one. A higher price next to weak case studies feels off. They will pass without telling you why.

## The resentment threshold

This is the most useful pricing exercise I have come across, attributed to Jule Kim. It is more honest than asking yourself "what do I feel confident charging?" Confidence lies. Resentment does not.

Before any pricing conversation, write down three numbers privately. Just for you. No one else needs to see them.

1. **The fair number.** The price where, if the client said yes, you would feel calm. Not greedy. Not stretched. The work is fair on both sides.
2. **The resentment number.** The price below which you would actually feel ripped off doing the work. You would start the project annoyed and the annoyance would leak into every revision call.
3. **The not-sure number.** The price at which you are honestly not certain you are worth it yet. Where you would deliver, but with low-grade anxiety the whole way through.

Your real working floor is the resentment number, plus a small margin for the bad days that show up in every project. Anything below the floor is poison. Resentment from day one is a cost the project pays in scope creep, missed deadlines, and a tone you cannot quite hide.

The fair number is your target. The not-sure number is your aspiration, not your working price. If you find yourself quoting at the not-sure number, ask why. Either the buyer is bigger than your usual fit and you are scared, or you are quoting from ego, not from preparation.

**Example.** You are quoting a brand identity. You write down:

- Fair: 12,000.
- Resentment: 7,500.
- Not sure: 18,000.

The buyer offers 6,000. You know to walk away, because below 7,500 the project poisons itself. You quote 12,000 with a clear scope. If the buyer pushes back hard, your ceiling for negotiation is somewhere around 9,000 to 10,000, never below 8,000. You leave the call without that low-grade dread.

## A small protection move: bring up the price first

This one is short but matters. If you wait for the client to ask about budget, you have already lost the price conversation. You are answering. You should be naming.

Bring up price early. Not at the end of a long pitch. Early enough that the buyer has not yet built up an expectation in their head that you will then have to dismantle.

A short anchor.

**You:** "Before we go further, projects of this kind tend to be in the [range] range. I want to mention it now so we are on the same page before we get deeper."

You do not need to apologize. You do not need to justify. State, then stop. The buyer either says "that works" or starts a real conversation about scope. Both are useful. Surprise at the end of a proposal is not.

## Try This

Pick the next quote you are about to send. Spend fifteen minutes:

1. Write your three numbers. Fair, resentment, not-sure. Be honest. No one is reading.
2. Compare them to what you were about to quote. Is the planned quote above or below your resentment line?
3. If it is below, raise it to at least your fair number. Do not negotiate against yourself before the client even sees the number.
4. Write one sentence to introduce the price early in the conversation, not at the end.
5. Send the quote. Note what happens. Add the result to your notes for next time.

This is how you build pricing muscle. Same way as physical training. Small, repeated, slightly heavier than last time.

## Summary

Most price increases fail because they happen too fast or do not happen at all. Doubling is the right step size. The price moves together with positioning, case studies, and your ability to say the number without flinching. The resentment threshold gives you a real floor: the price below which the work poisons itself from day one. Naming the number early protects you from surprise at the end. None of this is about charging more for the sake of it. It is about charging in a way you can actually defend, deliver, and live with.
