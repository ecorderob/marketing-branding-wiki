## Why people resist your conclusions

Watch what happens when someone delivers a hard decision without showing how they got there.

"We are letting the contractor go." "We are pausing this project." "We need to renegotiate the timeline." The other person hears the conclusion and immediately starts arguing with it. Even when the decision is right. Even when they would have agreed if they had seen the reasoning.

The conclusion is not the problem. The missing reasoning is the problem.

This page is about a small, useful frame for difficult conversations. You show the whole train, not just the last car. People can follow your thinking, ask questions, and arrive at the conclusion with you instead of resisting it from outside.

## The train metaphor

A simple way to picture how communication should flow when stakes are high.

> **Definition:** The train. Engine, cars, caboose. Engine is your honest feeling about the situation. Cars are the sequence of thoughts that connect feeling to conclusion. Caboose is the conclusion or proposal.

Most people deliver only the caboose. The conclusion arrives at the station with no engine and no cars to explain how it got there. The listener has to invent the missing pieces, often in a way that makes you look unfair, arbitrary, or sudden.

When you show the whole train, the listener gets to ride along. They see the feeling that started it. They see the considerations you weighed. By the time the conclusion arrives, it does not surprise them. They may still disagree with parts of it, but they cannot accuse you of being arbitrary.

## Starting with the engine

The hardest part for most professionals is starting with how you actually feel.

Business culture trains us to skip this. Open with the conclusion. Skip the messy part. Be efficient. The result is that conversations about hard things feel cold and clinical, which makes them harder, not easier.

A small move that helps. Open with a short, honest feeling statement.

- "I have been dreading this conversation."
- "I am uncomfortable bringing this up."
- "I am genuinely torn about this."

One sentence. No long preamble. No apology. Just the truth about your state.

The other person almost always softens immediately. The clinical version triggers their defenses. The honest version triggers their empathy. Same content. Different door.

> **Tip:** The fear is that admitting discomfort makes you look weak. It does the opposite. The person who can name their own state in the first sentence comes across as the most grounded person in the room.

## Showing the cars

The middle of the train is the part most often skipped. The sequence of thoughts that connects the feeling to the conclusion.

Most people compress this into a single sentence: "After thinking about it, we decided to..." That sentence does no work. The listener cannot see what was thought, and so cannot follow.

The fix is to slow down and narrate, briefly. You do not need to recreate every consideration. Two or three honest beats are enough.

- "I have been looking at the remaining tasks against the budget we have left."
- "And I noticed we are spending more time on revisions than on the new pages."
- "When I projected that out, the math does not work."

Three short sentences. The listener now sees what you saw. The conclusion that follows is not a surprise.

This is not theater. The cars are real. They are the actual thoughts you had on the way to the decision. You are just letting the listener ride along instead of arriving at the conclusion alone.

## Landing the caboose

Once the engine and cars are out, the conclusion is almost easy.

> "So I think we should pause the second phase and reset the scope."

Short. Clear. Specific. No hedging. The conclusion lands clean because everything that supports it has already been laid down.

A common mistake at this point. Continuing to talk after the conclusion. Adding caveats. Defending the decision before anyone has objected.

Resist that. Deliver the conclusion. Stop. Let the silence sit. Let the other person respond. The temptation to keep talking is anxiety. The discipline is to let it end.

> **Warning:** If you keep adding caveats after the conclusion, the other person starts looking for things to argue with. The longer you talk, the more reasons you give them. The shortest version of the conclusion is the strongest version.

## Avoiding business jargon in the cars

A small but important detail. The cars of the train should be in human language, not business language.

Compare:

- Jargoned: "We need to align cross-functional stakeholders before we can ratify the recommendation."
- Plain: "I want to make sure the design team and the engineering lead agree before we lock this in."

The first one sounds fine but communicates almost nothing. The second one is shorter, more honest, and the listener can act on it.

When you slip into business jargon mid-sentence, you are usually doing one of two things. Performing. Or hiding. Neither helps. Plain language tells the listener exactly what you are seeing.

## A worked example

The same hard conversation, two ways.

**Caboose only:**

> "We have decided to end the engagement at the end of this month. We will provide all current files and a final invoice. Thanks for the work so far."

The reader walks away cold. They do not know if it is them, the budget, the project, the company, or something else. They will fill in the worst possible explanation.

**Whole train:**

> "I have been dreading this conversation, and I want to be straight with you about it.
>
> When we started, we scoped the project around a specific outcome. Over the last three months, the brief has shifted to something different, and the budget has not moved with it. I have been trying to find a way to make the new shape work, and I cannot get the math to land.
>
> So I am going to wind down the engagement at the end of this month. I will hand over all the files and send a final invoice. I want to do this cleanly and leave the door open if the project comes back to its original shape later."

Same decision. Different conversation. The reader can disagree with parts of the train. They cannot say the conclusion came out of nowhere.

## Transparent response to scope creep

A specific place the train metaphor pays off. The middle of a project, when the client adds something to the scope.

The wrong responses are common. Passive acceptance: "Sure, we can do that." Then resentment builds. Defensive pushback: "That is not in scope." Then the client feels handled, not heard.

The transparent response is short and structured.

> "That is an interesting idea. It expands on our original plan. Let's map out what that involves and how we can best integrate it."

Then jointly work through the impact on timeline, budget, and the core objective. The client sees you taking the idea seriously, sees the cost, and can decide whether the trade is worth it. The decision becomes shared.

## Transparent response to creative feedback

Another place the same approach works. When a client gives feedback on creative work.

The lazy response is to nod and execute. The defensive response is to push back. Both miss the point of the feedback, which is usually about a goal the client has not stated clearly.

A small transparent response that opens the conversation up.

> "I can make those revisions. To make sure I am on the right track, can you tell me more about what you are hoping these changes will achieve?"

Half the time, the conversation that follows reveals that the requested change does not actually serve the goal. The client realizes it themselves. You did not have to argue. You just made the goal visible.

## Try This

Pick one hard conversation you have been avoiding. Spend ten minutes on this:

1. Write the caboose. The conclusion you want to deliver. One sentence.
2. Write the engine. Your honest feeling about the situation. One sentence.
3. Write three cars. The actual thoughts that connect the feeling to the conclusion. Plain language, no jargon.
4. Read it aloud. Does each sentence sound like something you would actually say?
5. Schedule the conversation. Open with the engine. Move through the cars. Land the caboose. Stop.

The conversation will not be easy. It will be much shorter than the version you have been imagining.

## Summary

People resist conclusions they cannot follow. The fix is to show the whole train, not just the caboose. Open with how you actually feel. Walk through two or three honest considerations that led you here. Land the conclusion in one short sentence and stop talking. Use plain language, not business jargon, in the middle. Apply the same shape to scope creep and creative feedback. The conversations that used to feel adversarial start to feel collaborative. Your conclusions stop being defended and start being understood.
