## The quiet pattern in every room

Walk into any meeting with senior people in it and watch who talks the most. Then watch who is being listened to.

They are usually not the same person. The person doing most of the talking is often nervous, junior, or trying to prove something. The person being listened to says short, clear things, then waits.

This is not about being unfriendly or aloof. It is about choosing your words. People who use fewer words for the same idea sound more in control. People who use many words for a small idea sound the opposite.

This page is about the small habits that cut your word count without cutting your point.

## Why brevity reads as power

There is a simple physical reason. Words take time. Time costs the listener attention. When you use a lot of words to make a small point, the listener has to do the work of figuring out which parts mattered.

When you use a few words to make a clear point, the listener gets it immediately. They have spare attention. They start thinking about your idea, not about parsing your sentences.

The speaker who saves the listener work is the speaker who feels easier to follow, sharper, more credible. None of this is conscious. It just happens.

> **Tip:** Watch any senior leader you respect for ten minutes. Count how often they speak. Count how often they pause. The pattern is almost always the same. Less talk. More pause. Each sentence carries weight because there are not many of them.

## The high-power and low-power patterns

The two ways of speaking are easy to spot, once you know what to look for.

**High-power communicators tend to:**

- Speak slowly.
- Use few words for an idea.
- Pause between thoughts.
- Ask direct questions.
- Make statements without asking permission first.
- Drop hedge words like "maybe", "I think", "sort of".

**Low-power communicators tend to:**

- Speak quickly.
- Use many words for a simple idea.
- Fill silences out of discomfort.
- Ask permission before saying things.
- Pad statements with hedges.

You can probably hear yourself in both lists. Most people are a mix. The point is not to become a robot. It is to notice when you are slipping into the second list and pull yourself back to the first.

> **Warning:** "Confident" does not mean "loud" or "certain". It means "willing to let a sentence end without padding". You can be soft-spoken and still high-power. The volume is not the signal. The shape of the sentence is.

## Hedge words to drop

A short list of phrases that quietly weaken almost any sentence.

- "I think..."
- "Maybe we should..."
- "Sort of..."
- "Just a thought, but..."
- "I could be wrong, but..."
- "Does that make sense?"

You do not have to remove every one of them. They have a place when you genuinely are uncertain or genuinely want input. The problem is when they appear by reflex.

A useful experiment. Record yourself in a normal work conversation for ten minutes. Listen to it. Count how often you opened a sentence with "I think" or closed it with "if that makes sense". Almost everyone is shocked the first time.

The fix is not to memorize a different opener. It is to notice the reflex and let the silence sit.

**Example.**

- Hedged: "I just think maybe we should sort of consider going with option two, if that makes sense."
- Direct: "Option two is the right call."

The hedged version has more words and less authority. The direct version is shorter and clearer, and the listener can disagree if they want. The hedged version invites argument; the direct version invites response.

## The Answer-Reason-Detail format

A small structure that prevents rambling on almost any answer or update.

> **Definition:** Answer-Reason-Detail. State the conclusion first. Then give the reason. Then offer detail only if asked.

Most people do this in reverse. They walk through the context, then build to the answer. By the time the answer arrives, the listener is tired. Worse, the listener has been guessing the whole time.

A status update in Answer-Reason-Detail.

- **Answer.** "We are two days behind."
- **Reason.** "Client feedback came in late on Friday."
- **Detail (only if asked).** "Specifically, the second round of revisions came in Monday morning instead of last Wednesday, and that pushed the design phase by a couple of days."

The listener gets the headline immediately. They can decide whether they need the detail. Most of the time, they do not.

This format also makes you a better question-answerer. Direct questions get direct answers. "Yes, it is on track." "No, we are two days late." Then a one-line reason. Done.

> **Tip:** Most meetings would be 30 percent shorter if everyone in them used Answer-Reason-Detail. You cannot make everyone do it. You can make yourself do it.

## Be precise enough that no one else has to translate

A request that is vague forces the other person to spend their attention guessing what you actually want. They have nothing left for the work itself.

> "Can you pass the salt?" is instantly actionable.
> "This doesn't taste right" requires guessing, follow-ups, and interpretation.

Precision is a form of respect for the other person's attention.

A useful test, before sending any meaningful request. Could the recipient act on this immediately, or would they need to ask one or more clarifying questions first?

If they would need to ask, the request is not ready. Tighten it. Say what you actually want, who it is for, and by when. Keep refining until the message is one short paragraph and the action is unmistakable. *Source: Jule Kim.*

## When more words help

Brevity is the default. It is not always right.

A few situations where extended explanation is the better choice.

- **Creative presentations.** The client needs to follow how you arrived at the idea, not just see the final image.
- **Training and onboarding.** Skipping the explanation to look senior makes the new person guess. That is expensive.
- **Complex problem-solving.** Sometimes the right move is to think out loud and let the team see your reasoning.
- **Difficult conversations.** Showing your thinking, not just your conclusion, builds trust. (More on this in **05. Show the Train, Not Just the Caboose**.)

The rule is not "always be short". The rule is "be the shortest version of useful". Status update? Short. Strategy presentation? Long enough to land. The mistake is using one default for both.

## Why over-explaining happens

When you catch yourself over-explaining, the cause is usually one of three things.

1. **Insecurity.** You are trying to make a weak answer feel stronger by adding more words. The extra words make it sound less sure, not more.
2. **Ego.** You want to display knowledge. The display itself signals you needed to display it.
3. **Lack of context awareness.** You are using the same communication style in a context that called for a different one.

Before launching into a long explanation, run a quick check. Am I doing this because the situation calls for it, or because I am uncomfortable? If it is the second one, stop and let the silence sit. The silence is doing useful work.

## Try This

Pick one meeting today. Before the meeting, write Answer-Reason-Detail at the top of your notepad.

In the meeting, do two things.

1. Every time you give a status update or answer a direct question, lead with the answer. One sentence. Then the reason. Stop. Wait to see if anyone asks for the detail. Most of the time, they will not.
2. Once during the meeting, catch yourself starting to hedge. Stop mid-sentence and finish without the hedge. ("I think maybe..." becomes "...".) Notice the listener's reaction.

After the meeting, count how many times you used "I think" in the first half versus the second half. The shift is the practice.

## Summary

Brevity is not coldness. It is the practice of choosing words that respect the listener's attention. The high-power pattern is slow, short, and unhedged. The low-power pattern is fast, long, and apologetic. Drop the hedges. Use Answer-Reason-Detail for any update or answer. Make requests precise enough that no one has to translate them. Use longer explanations when the situation calls for them, not when discomfort is pushing you to fill silence. The change is small. The effect on how you are perceived is large.
