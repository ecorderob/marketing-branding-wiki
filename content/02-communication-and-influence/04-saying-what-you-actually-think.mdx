## The gap that costs you

There is a gap between what you actually think and what you say in professional conversations.

Sometimes the gap is small. A polite tweak. A softer word. Sometimes the gap is large. You hold a real concern, swallow it, and walk out of the meeting having agreed to something you did not believe in. That gap, repeated over months, is where most professional resentment, drift, and quiet failures live.

This page is about closing the gap carefully. Not by becoming blunt. By becoming honest in a way that people can hear.

## Say what you think, on purpose

Blair Enns has a principle that is worth quoting plainly: if you could make one rule for professional life, it would be that you must say what you think.

Most people hold back. They hold back because of cultural conditioning. Because they are afraid of judgment. Because they defer to authority. Because they are worried about being liked. The result is conversations that feel evasive on both sides. The other person can sense that you are not really telling them what you think. They stop trusting the version of you that shows up in meetings.

The people who get described as "straight shooters" are not unusually brave. They are simply doing what everyone should be doing by default.

Before any important conversation, ask yourself two questions.

- "What do I actually think about this?"
- "What is holding me back from saying it?"

Name the obstacle out loud, in your head. Then say the thing anyway. *Source: Blair Enns / Jule Kim.*

> **Tip:** Most of the time, the obstacle is smaller than you fear. The other person almost never reacts as badly as your brain predicted. You leave most "scary" conversations relieved you said the thing.

## The online version of the same problem

If you struggle to say what you think out loud, you almost certainly do the same thing in writing. You soften posts. You walk back opinions in the comments. You avoid topics that would upset some part of your audience.

The online hesitancy is not a separate phenomenon. It is the same thing showing up in a different medium. It is downstream of how you communicate offline.

You cannot fix the online version without addressing the root. Practice saying what you think in low-stakes face-to-face conversations first. The online voice will firm up as a side effect.

## Define the outcome before you speak

Before saying something that is emotionally charged or socially risky, decide what outcome you actually want from saying it.

This sounds obvious. Most people skip it. They feel the urge to speak, they speak, and only afterward do they realize they wanted advice but expressed frustration, or they wanted permission but framed it as a complaint. The right thing said in the wrong shape gets the wrong response.

A short check, before any meaningful statement.

1. **What do I want to happen as a result of saying this?**
2. **Is this the right person to say it to?**
3. **Am I packaging this in a way that gives them a realistic chance of helping me?**

These questions take ten seconds. They change the sentence you are about to use.

**Example.**

You are frustrated that a project keeps changing scope. You feel the urge to vent to your colleague.

- Without the check: "This project is impossible. Every week the brief shifts."
- With the check: "I want help thinking through how to push back on the latest scope change. Can you walk through it with me?"

Same situation. Different outcome. The first one buys you sympathy. The second one buys you progress.

*Source: Jule Kim.*

## Smart phrases for not knowing

A specific case where most people lie. The moment you do not know the answer.

The reflex is to bluff. To pad. To suggest you knew but were going to say something more nuanced. Clients and teammates can almost always tell.

Replacing the bluff with a small set of honest phrases is one of the highest-return communication moves you can make. They show you are confident enough in your problem-solving that you do not need to fake.

Memorize three or four that fit your voice.

- "I want to make sure I understand this correctly..."
- "Help me think through this with you..."
- "I do not want to guess what you need..."
- "This is new for us, so we will figure it out together..."
- "I do not know yet. Give me until the end of the day."

These phrases sound vulnerable. They actually signal the opposite. The person who can say "I do not know" without flinching is the person who is least afraid of being wrong, which is the person you trust most.

> **Warning:** Smart phrases stop being smart the moment they become a tic. If you say "help me think through this" five times in one meeting, it stops being honest and becomes a stalling pattern. Use them when they are true.

## The power of the pause

Tied to the above. When asked a difficult question, you are allowed to think before answering.

Most people answer too fast. They feel the pressure of silence and start talking before they know what they are going to say. The first sentence has to be edited by the second sentence. By sentence five, the answer has wandered.

The fix is one short phrase, said calmly.

> "That is a good question. Let me think for a moment."

Then think. Five seconds. Ten seconds. Most rooms can handle it. The pause does not signal ignorance. It signals that you take the question seriously enough to give it a real answer.

People tend to remember the answer that came after a thoughtful pause more than the one that came in a rush.

## A four-step filter for requests

Most people say yes by reflex. The yes feels generous in the moment. A few hours later, they realize they have committed to something they cannot do, do not want to do, or never were the right person for.

Before responding to what feels like a request, run a four-step check.

1. **Is this actually a request?** A complaint is not a request. A vent is not a request. A statement of fact is not a request. Sometimes the other person is just talking. They do not want you to act.
2. **Do I have the authority and capacity?** If you do not, a yes commits you to delivering something you cannot deliver, which is worse than a no.
3. **Do I want to do this? Does it align with what I am trying to do?** If the answer is no, you owe yourself the honesty of noticing.
4. **What is my actual response?** Yes. No. Yes-with-conditions. Not me, but here is who. An alternative.

You do not have to give the answer immediately. "Let me check my calendar and get back to you by end of day" is a respectable response to almost any request that arrives in real time.

> **Tip:** The most common mistake is treating a complaint as a request. Someone vents about a problem; you start solving it. They did not want a solution. They wanted to be heard. Listen first. Confirm what they actually want before responding.

## When to say it, when to hold it

Saying what you think is not a license to dump every thought on every person. The filter still matters.

Three checks before sharing a hard truth.

- **Does sharing this serve the situation?** Some thoughts are accurate and unhelpful at the same time.
- **Is the timing right?** The same sentence lands differently in front of a board than it does over coffee.
- **Is my intention constructive?** If you are about to say something to score a point, hold it. If you are saying it to move the situation forward, say it.

These checks take seconds. They are the difference between honest communication and reckless communication. Honest communication serves the relationship. Reckless communication uses honesty as cover for a hit.

## Try This

Pick one thing you have been avoiding saying to someone at work. A small concern. A push-back. A real opinion you have softened.

Spend five minutes on this:

1. Write down what you actually think. Plain words.
2. Write down what you have been saying instead.
3. Write down what is holding you back from saying the real version.
4. Run the three-step filter. Does sharing serve the situation? Is the timing right? Is my intention constructive?
5. If all three are yes, say it the next time the conversation comes up. Use one of the smart phrases if it helps. Pause before answering follow-ups.

Notice what happens to the relationship over the next week. In most cases, it gets better, not worse.

## Summary

The gap between what you think and what you say is where most professional friction quietly grows. Close it on purpose. Say what you think after a short check that the timing and intention serve the situation. Define the outcome you want before you speak. Use smart phrases when you do not know the answer instead of bluffing. Pause before answering hard questions. Filter incoming requests through four steps before saying yes. None of this makes you blunt. It makes you honest in a shape people can actually hear.
