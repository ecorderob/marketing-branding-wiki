## Why questions are the lever

The income gap between people doing similar work is mostly a communication gap. And the communication gap is mostly a question gap.

Bad questions get short answers. Short answers get bad work. The whole project is shaped by the questions you ask in the first hour.

Good questions get the other person to think out loud. They surface what was hidden. They align the work with the actual goal. The whole project gets cheaper, faster, and better when the right questions land in the first conversation.

This page is a small set of questions you can use anywhere. A meeting. A kickoff. A first call. A check-in with a teammate.

## The question hierarchy

Not all questions are equal. They sit on a spectrum, from low value to high.

1. **Yes/No questions.** "Do you like this?" Almost no information.
2. **Multiple choice.** "Do you prefer A or B?" Gets a choice but limits the thinking.
3. **Open information.** "What is your budget?" Gets a fact, not an insight.
4. **Discovery questions.** "What would make this design successful for your audience?" Helps the other person think more deeply.
5. **Strategic questions.** "If this project succeeds, what becomes possible that is not possible today?" Reframes the whole situation.

Most professionals live in the bottom three. They ask facts. They get facts. They never get to the conversation that changes the project.

The shift is small. Move one rung up. "Do you like this design?" becomes "What would make this design successful?". Same topic. Different planet.

> **Tip:** You do not need to abandon the bottom rungs. Yes/no questions are still useful for confirming details. Just stop using them as the main mode of inquiry.

## The Three Golden Questions

If you remember nothing else from this page, remember these three. Used together, they give you the shape of any project, in any context.

1. **"What are you trying to achieve?"**
2. **"What is stopping you from achieving it?"**
3. **"How will you know when you have succeeded?"**

The first reveals goals. The second reveals real obstacles, which are almost never what the brief says. The third reveals success criteria, which is the part that lets you ship the work and have the client agree it is done.

You can ask these in a kickoff. You can ask them in a one-on-one with a teammate. You can ask them of yourself before starting any meaningful piece of work.

**Example.**

**You:** What are you trying to achieve with this rebrand?

**Client:** We want to look more premium so we can charge more.

**You:** What is stopping you from charging more today?

**Client:** Honestly, half our team thinks our prices are already too high.

**You:** How will you know when this rebrand has succeeded?

**Client:** When the next ten proposals close at the higher rate.

You learned more in three questions than the original brief contained. The client also just told you exactly how the project will be measured.

## Leading questions versus discovery questions

There is a kind of question that feels useful but kills trust over time. The leading question.

A leading question already contains the answer the asker wants. "Don't you think we should go with option B?" "Wouldn't it be better if we just simplified this?" The other person can sense it. They feel pushed. They either agree out of politeness or push back out of stubbornness. Either way, the conversation gets worse.

> **Definition:** Leading question. A question framed in a way that makes one answer obvious or socially expected. Feels like steering, even when no one calls it out.

A discovery question opens space instead of closing it. Same topic, different shape.

- Leading: "Don't you think a simpler layout would be better?"
- Discovery: "If we made the layout simpler, what would we gain or lose?"

The discovery version gets a real answer. The leading version gets a polite yes that you cannot trust.

## Suitcase words

Some words look like they are saying one thing. They are actually carrying many different things, packed inside the same word, and each person opens the suitcase differently.

Kevin Daly calls these "suitcase words." The classic offenders in creative work are everywhere. *Premium. Modern. Clean. Innovative. Bold. Friendly. Professional.*

Each of these words means something different to every person who uses it. If a client says they want something "premium" and you nod, you are agreeing to a definition that may have nothing to do with theirs.

The fix is to unpack the suitcase, in real time. Two questions, asked plainly.

> "When you say [word], what does that mean specifically to you?"
> "Can you give me an example of something that has that quality?"

You ask these without irony. Most clients are happy to answer. Until you ask, they have not actually thought about what the word meant. Sometimes they answer and surprise themselves.

> **Warning:** Skipping this step is the source of most "the client did not like it" surprises three weeks into a project. By then, the suitcase has been open for too long, and you cannot rewind. Unpack early.

## Questions that surface what is hidden

Clients often hold back the most useful information. Concerns they think will sound silly. Hopes they think are too big. Constraints they are embarrassed by. They will not volunteer this. You have to ask for it.

A few questions that work, especially after you have built some trust in the conversation.

- "Is there anything you are not telling me because you think I will not understand it?"
- "What would you do if money was not an issue?"
- "What are people saying privately about this project?"
- "What are you hoping for that feels too big to ask for?"

You will not get a useful answer to all of them every time. You will get a useful answer to one of them most times. That one answer often changes the project.

> **Tip:** These questions only work after the client trusts you a little. Asking them in the first ten minutes feels invasive. Asking them in minute thirty feels generous.

## Ask, do not assume

A small habit that costs nothing and saves weeks. Ask about the things you would normally guess.

Service providers routinely make assumptions about the client's vision, budget, competitive context, and constraints, and then act on those assumptions without checking. This is both inefficient and quietly disrespectful. The client knows their context better than you do. Ask them.

Useful low-friction questions for any new conversation:

- "What inspired this?"
- "What would a home run look like?"
- "How closely do we need to follow the precedent?"
- "When you say radically different, how different are we talking?"

The questions are simple. That is why they work. They sound like real curiosity, which is what they are, and they save the project from the assumption you would have made.

## Try This

Pick the next meaningful conversation on your calendar. Before it, write down three questions. The first should be a Golden Question. The second should be a discovery question, not a leading one. The third should target a likely suitcase word for that conversation. ("When you say modern, what does that mean to you?")

During the meeting, ask all three. Watch what happens to the conversation after each one.

After the meeting, write down the answer to the third question. The answer to that one is usually worth the whole meeting.

## Summary

Most professional conversations stay on the bottom rungs of the question ladder, where information is shallow. Move up one rung and the work changes. The three Golden Questions cover most projects. Discovery questions open space where leading questions close it. Suitcase words look agreed upon and are not, so unpack them in real time. And when you would normally assume, ask. Better questions cost nothing and save the entire project.
