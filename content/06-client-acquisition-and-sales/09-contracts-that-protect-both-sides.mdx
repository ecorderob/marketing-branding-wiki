## Why the contract is part of the relationship

Most creatives treat the contract as an annoying afterthought. Something the lawyer handles. Something to get past so the real work can start.

That is a mistake. The contract is part of the relationship. The shape of it tells the client, very clearly, what kind of professional they are working with. A contract that says "trust me, we will figure it out" trains the client to treat the work the same way. A contract that is clean and specific trains them to respect it.

This page is about the three rules that keep creative contracts healthy, and what to do when scope inevitably tries to grow.

## Rule 1: Fixed-bid, not hourly

Hourly billing seems fair on paper. You bill what you work. The client pays for what you do. Nobody gets cheated.

In practice, hourly billing punishes you for being good at your job. The faster you work, the less you make. The slower you work, the more you make. The client also has no idea what the project will cost, which makes them anxious through the whole engagement.

Fixed-bid pricing flips this.

> **Definition:** Fixed-bid. A single agreed price for a clearly defined scope of work. The client pays the same number whether the work takes ten hours or fifty.

Fixed-bid does three things at once.

- It forces both parties to define the scope before signing.
- It rewards your speed and experience instead of penalizing them.
- It puts the financial risk of poor estimating on you, which makes you a better estimator.

The first time you switch from hourly to fixed-bid, you will probably lose money on a project or two. That is the tuition for getting better at scoping. After three or four projects, your fixed-bid prices end up higher than your hourly would have been, and the client is happier because they know the number.

> **Warning:** "Fixed-bid plus changes" is fine. "Fixed-bid plus hourly for anything not in scope" is fine. "Fixed-bid that quietly drifts into hourly because nobody wrote down what was in scope" is the disaster you are trying to avoid.

## Rule 2: 50% upfront, 50% on delivery

This rule applies to every project, regardless of size. It is not about the money. It is about commitment, on both sides.

The 50% upfront does two things. It filters out clients who are not serious. People who would not pay half upfront are usually the same people who would not pay at all. It also funds the work, so you are not floating the project on your own savings.

The 50% on delivery does two things. It keeps the client engaged in the closing phase. They have a reason to review, accept, and sign off. It also gives you a clean handover, where the work is reviewed and paid for in one moment, not stretched across weeks of "almost done".

Some practices break this into thirds for longer engagements. 33% upfront, 33% at midpoint, 33% on delivery. That is fine. The principle is the same. Do not finish the work before you get paid for the work.

> **Tip:** Clients sometimes push for "net 30 from delivery" or longer. That is a request for free credit. You can grant it for clients you trust. Charge for it. Or politely decline.

## Rule 3: Deliverables defined with precision

The clause in a contract that prevents the most fights is the deliverables clause. The clause that causes the most fights is the same clause when it is vague.

Vague: "Brand strategy."

Specific: "Brand strategy document including market analysis, competitive positioning, messaging hierarchy, and visual direction guidelines, delivered as a single PDF, with one round of revisions included."

The specific version is harder to write. It also makes scope creep almost impossible. When the client asks for something extra, you can hold up the document and point to what was not in it. The conversation becomes about a change order, not about what you owe them.

A simple test. Read your deliverables clause aloud and imagine the client interpreting each phrase as broadly as possible. Anywhere they could expand the scope by reading generously, tighten the wording.

**Example.**

A studio agreed to "design a website" for a fixed price. The client interpreted "design" as including copy, photography direction, hosting setup, and three post-launch revision rounds. The studio thought "design" meant designing the screens. Three months later, both parties were furious. Both were right, given how the contract was written.

The next contract said: "Design five page templates (home, services, about, blog, contact), responsive across desktop and mobile, delivered as Figma files. Excludes copywriting, photography, and engineering. Includes two rounds of design revisions. Additional rounds billed at [rate]."

The same project. No fight.

## How to handle scope creep

Scope creep is not a sign of a bad client. It is a sign that the project is alive. The client sees the work taking shape and starts having ideas. Some of those ideas are good. None of them are free.

The right response to a scope-creep request is not "no" and not "yes". It is structured.

1. **Acknowledge.** "That is a great idea."
2. **Quantify.** "Adding it would be roughly [X] additional hours, which would shift the timeline to [date] and the price to [number]."
3. **Offer options.**
   - "We can add it to this project at the new number."
   - "We can defer it to phase 2."
   - "I can refer you to someone if you want it done separately."

This response does three things. It validates the client's instinct. It puts the cost in front of them, where they can decide. It avoids the trap of saying yes for free or saying no flat.

A simple sentence to keep in your head:

> "Anything not in the scope is a change order. Change orders are fine. They have prices and timelines."

Say that out loud the next time a client asks for something extra. Most clients respect the structure immediately. The ones who do not are showing you something useful about how the project would have gone anyway.

> **Warning:** The temptation to say yes to small additions is strong. Each one feels minor. They compound. Six "small" additions across a project equal a quarter of the original budget, given away.

## Vendor versus strategic partner, in writing

The contract is the place where the difference between a vendor relationship and a strategic partnership becomes physical. You can read someone's contract and tell, before meeting them, which one they are.

A vendor contract:

- Receives a detailed brief.
- Specifies outputs, not outcomes.
- Has tight, low-trust revision controls.
- Ends at delivery.

A strategic partner contract:

- Specifies outcomes the work is meant to support.
- Includes diagnostic or strategy phases.
- Has clear handovers between phases.
- Often includes an ongoing relationship clause for follow-on work.

You do not need to use the second style for every project. Use it for clients where the work is part of a bigger goal, and the relationship is meant to last.

> **Tip:** A strategic partner contract is not longer or more legalistic. It is just oriented toward outcomes the client cares about, written in plain language, with diagnostic and execution phases that can be priced separately.

## Try This

Pull out your last three contracts. For each one, do the following:

1. Read the deliverables clause aloud. Note the places where a client could read it broadly and add scope.
2. Check whether the payment terms match the 50/50 rule. If you are running net-30 with no upfront, ask yourself why.
3. Check whether you wrote it as a fixed-bid or quietly let it drift into hourly.

Pick one of the three and rewrite the deliverables clause until you would be confident defending it in a scope dispute. Use that clause as the new template for your next project.

## Summary

The contract is part of the work. Fixed-bid pricing rewards your skill instead of punishing it. 50% upfront and 50% on delivery filters out non-buyers and funds the work. Deliverables defined with precision prevent the fights that ruin good relationships. Scope creep is normal and not a problem, as long as every change becomes a change order with a price and a timeline. The contract you write is also a quiet signal of which kind of professional you are. Make it match the practice you are trying to build.
