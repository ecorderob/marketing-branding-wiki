## Not every prospect is a client

Every full pipeline has a hidden tax. Some of the people in it are not buyers. They are just having a conversation with you because it is free.

You usually find this out three weeks in, after the proposal, after the second call, after a small bit of free advice. They go quiet. They reappear with one more question. The deal never closes. The hours are gone.

The fix is to spot the wrong-fit prospect in the first ten minutes, before you have invested anything that hurts to lose. This page is the simplest version of that filter.

## Three archetypes you will meet

In the first call, every prospect tends to fall into one of three groups. The signs are easy to spot once you know what you are looking for.

### Partners (this is who you want)

Partners are the real buyers. They show up wanting a solution and willing to do the work. They:

- Describe the objective, not just the problem.
- Give you an honest read on what they have already tried.
- Acknowledge their own role in the situation.
- Ask follow-up questions about your approach.
- Lean in when you offer a strategic insight.

You can feel the difference. The conversation has weight. You are not pulling answers out of them.

### Storytellers (politely walk away)

Storytellers want sympathy, not a solution. They have a long story about how hard their situation has been. They:

- Return to the problem when you ask about the outcome.
- Explain why nothing they have tried has worked.
- Blame external factors for everything.
- Seem to enjoy the drama of the situation.
- Resist any new thinking you offer.

A storyteller will not become a partner during this project. They are not looking for a fix. They are looking for company in their problem. Refer them on. Wish them well. Move on.

> **Warning:** Storytellers can be charming. The story is often genuinely interesting. That is the trap. Interesting does not mean buying.

### Affirmation-seekers (low priority)

Affirmation-seekers have already decided what they want. They are looking for someone to execute it. They:

- Ask you to make their idea, not advise on it.
- Dismiss alternative approaches.
- Want execution, not expertise.
- See advisors as order-takers.

You can sometimes work with affirmation-seekers, but only if you are willing to be a vendor. If you are trying to be a strategic partner, this is the wrong client. They will fight every reframe. The work suffers. So does the relationship.

## The three-question test

You can sort most prospects into the right bucket with three questions, asked in the first ten minutes of the first call.

1. **"What does a successful outcome look like to you?"**
   - Partners describe an objective.
   - Storytellers go back to the problem.

2. **"What have you tried so far?"**
   - Partners give an honest read.
   - Storytellers explain why nothing works.

3. **"What is the biggest thing that needs to change for this to improve?"**
   - Partners acknowledge their own role.
   - Storytellers blame external factors.

You do not need all three to come back perfectly. One bad answer can be a bad day. Two out of three pointing the same way is a strong signal.

> **Tip:** Keep the questions simple and ask them in a calm tone. They are diagnostic, not interrogative. The point is to listen, not to grade.

## The strategic guidance test

There is a fourth move that catches affirmation-seekers, who often slip past the first three questions.

Early in the call, offer one small strategic reframe. Just a sentence. Then watch what happens.

- A real buyer leans in. They ask a follow-up. They turn the idea over.
- A skeptic pushes back, but they engage with the idea. That is fine. They are still thinking.
- A wall ignores it. They change the subject back to tactics. They restart the brief from the top.

The wall is the affirmation-seeker. They will never value your thinking, no matter how much of it you offer. The work on this project will be a long argument about whose idea is in charge.

**Example.**

**Client:** We need a brand refresh. New logo, new colors, the whole thing.

**You:** Understood. One thought before we get into the details. Sometimes the brand looks dated because the audience has shifted, not because the visuals are old. Has the customer profile changed at all in the last two years?

**Partner:** Actually, yes, we have been moving upmarket. That might be related. What did you have in mind?

**Wall:** Right, but for now we just want to nail the logo. Can we focus on that?

The partner is curious. The wall is closed. You learned more in two sentences than the rest of the call would have told you.

## What to do with each archetype

Once you have a read, the response is simple.

- **Partner.** Continue with the rest of the discovery. Plan the proposal. Move forward.
- **Storyteller.** Politely close the call. Recommend a coach, a therapist, or a different kind of help if you can. Do not send a proposal.
- **Affirmation-seeker.** If you are willing to be a vendor and the price is right, accept the work as a clean execution job. If not, refer them out.

The hardest part is not the diagnosis. It is acting on it. There is a strong pull to send the proposal anyway. To hope the storyteller will become a partner once you start. They almost never do.

> **Tip:** Track every project you wished you had not taken. Look back at your first-call notes for those clients. The signs were almost always there in the first ten minutes. Trust them next time.

## Try This

Look at your last five clients. For each one, write which archetype they were on the first call. Be honest.

Now look at your last five lost or dropped projects. Same exercise.

If you spot a pattern (a lot of storytellers slipping through, or a lot of walls being signed), the fix is not in your sales script. It is in which prospects you are letting reach the call. The next layer of work is filtering further upstream, in your content and in who you take meetings with.

## Summary

Most prospects are not buyers. The three archetypes (Partner, Storyteller, Affirmation-seeker) cover almost everyone you will meet on a first call. Three short questions plus one small strategic reframe will tell you which one is sitting across from you, usually within ten minutes. The hardest part is not diagnosing. It is being willing to walk away from the ones who are not buyers, even when they are pleasant to talk to. The pipeline is healthier when you protect your time.
