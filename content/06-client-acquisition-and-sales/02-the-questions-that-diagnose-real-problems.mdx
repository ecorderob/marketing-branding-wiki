## Why the questions matter more than the answers

A new client conversation usually goes badly because of the questions, not the answers.

Most creatives ask transactional questions. "What do you want it to look like?" "How many ideas would you like?" "When do you need this?" The client answers them. The conversation ends. A quote gets sent. The work begins. Nobody understands the actual problem.

The questions in this page do something different. They make the client think out loud. They put the urgency into words. They show you whether the project is real, and whether the client is.

You do not need many of them. You need the right ones, asked in the right order.

## Coaching questions, not order-taking questions

The simplest reframe is to swap each transactional question for a coaching one. The shape changes, but each one is a question you can ask in a calm, curious tone.

| Order-taking | Coaching |
|---|---|
| What do you want the design to look like? | What problem is this design trying to solve for the people who will see it? |
| How many ideas would you like? | What factors will help you decide which direction is most likely to succeed? |
| When do you need this? | What needs to happen after this project, and how does that affect the deadline? |
| What is your budget? | What would it be worth to your business if this works? |

The transactional version asks the client to specify a deliverable. The coaching version asks the client to think about the outcome. Same conversation. Different planet.

> **Tip:** You will notice that coaching questions feel slower. They are. That is the point. Slowness in this part of the call is professional rigor. It is not wasted time.

## The five diagnostic questions

If you remember nothing else, remember these five. Used together, they cover most of what you need to know in the first call.

1. **Strategic context.** "What is the core business problem making this a priority right now?"
2. **Market.** "If I were your ideal customer, why would I choose you over your top competitor?"
3. **Internal reality.** "What happens the day after this project launches? Who owns making it succeed?"
4. **Previous attempts.** "What have you tried before this?"
5. **Decision structure.** "Who else is involved in this decision?"

Ask them in this order. Listen more than you talk. Take notes. The client almost always reveals the real shape of the project somewhere in the answers.

> **Warning:** Resist the urge to jump to solutions while they are answering. The moment you start designing the brochure in your head, you stop hearing them. Stay in diagnosis until the picture is clear.

## Find the compelling event

Salespeople are usually in a hurry to talk about the future. The close. The work. The payment. They skip the most useful part of the conversation, which is the past.

Somewhere in the client's history, a problem built up until it hit a boiling point and forced action. That boiling point has a name. The compelling event.

> **Definition:** Compelling event. The specific thing that happened recently that made this conversation happen today instead of next quarter or never.

Without a compelling event, the project is not real yet. The client may want it. They may even budget it. But it usually drifts. The deadline slips. The work loses urgency. With a compelling event, the project has gravity.

You find it with one question, asked plainly. "What has happened that has caused this conversation to happen today? What is the event or situation that is driving this?"

Then you listen. The client will reveal deadlines, internal pressures, failed past attempts, and what is at stake if nothing changes. All of that goes into the proposal later. *Source: Kevin Daly, Socratic Selling.*

**Example.**

**You:** What has changed recently that made this a priority now?

**Client:** Honestly, our biggest competitor just rebranded. The board saw it last week. They asked me what we are doing about it.

That is the compelling event. The board, last week, the competitor's rebrand. You now know the deadline pressure, the internal politics, and the person whose neck is on the line. None of that was in the original brief.

## Sell pain relief, not products

Buyers buy to relieve pain. The more vividly the client feels the pain in your conversation, the more reasonable your price feels by comparison.

This sounds manipulative. It is not, if the pain is real. Your job is not to invent it. Your job is to slow down and let the client say it out loud, so they remember it is real.

After they describe the problem, ask follow-ups that deepen the weight.

- "What happens if this does not go well?"
- "What would that mean for your team?"
- "What has the impact been so far?"
- "If nothing changes, what does next year look like?"

Do not rush to your solution. Do not start framing the proposal in your head. Let the client articulate the full consequence of doing nothing.

When they have, the price for fixing it stops feeling expensive. A 50,000-dollar project sounds small when the alternative they just described is a quarter of lost revenue or losing a major account.

> **Warning:** This works only when the pain is real. If you are leading the witness to invent pain that is not there, the client feels it and the relationship dies. The questions are honest. The answers are theirs.

## If they say it, they own it

There is a small rule that changes how sales conversations land.

> If you are saying it, you are selling. If they are saying it, you are closing.

When you tell a client what their problem is, they argue with it. Even when you are right. They argue because it is your idea, not theirs.

When the client says it, they own it. They cannot argue with themselves. The deal closes itself.

Design your sales conversations around questions, not statements. Where are you today? Where do you want to be? What is in the way? Let the client do 70 to 80 percent of the talking. By the end of the call, they have described the problem, named the stakes, and told you what success looks like. They have sold themselves on doing the work.

Your job is to ask better questions, not give better answers.

## Ask, do not assume

A small habit that costs nothing and saves a lot of time. Ask about the things you would normally guess.

You do not know what inspired the project. Ask. You do not know what success looks like. Ask. You do not know how closely they want you to follow what they did before. Ask. You do not know what "different" means to them. Ask.

Useful questions to keep on a sticky note for any new business call:

- "What inspired this idea?"
- "What would a home run look like?"
- "How closely do we need to follow the precedent?"
- "When you say radically different, how different are we talking?"

These questions are simple. That is why they work. The client answers them honestly because they do not feel cornered.

## Try This

Take the next discovery call on your calendar. Before the call, write a list of every question you usually ask. Then beside each one, write the coaching version of the same question.

On the call, replace at least three of your usual questions with the coaching version. Pick one of the five diagnostic questions and ask it in the first ten minutes. Make sure you ask, in some form, what has happened recently that made this a priority now.

After the call, write down two things. What did you learn that you would not have learned with your old questions? Did the client behave differently when you asked the new ones?

## Summary

The questions you ask shape what kind of person you are in the room. Coaching questions surface the real problem and put the client in charge of the thinking. The five diagnostic questions cover most of what you need to know. Hunting for the compelling event tells you whether the project is real. Letting the client articulate the pain makes the price feel reasonable. The simplest rule is also the most powerful. If they say it, they own it. Stop selling. Start asking.
