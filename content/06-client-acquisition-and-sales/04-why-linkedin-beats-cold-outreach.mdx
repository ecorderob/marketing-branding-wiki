## The cold outreach trap

If you sell anything more expensive than a coffee, cold outreach is the slowest way to make it work.

Cold outreach treats sales as a numbers game. Send a thousand messages, get ten replies, close one deal. The math works on paper. In practice, it burns you out, fills your inbox with strangers, and trains you to write things you would never want to read yourself.

There is a better path for higher-ticket work. It is not a hack. It is a platform that, oddly, was built for exactly this. LinkedIn.

This page explains why LinkedIn keeps outperforming the alternatives for high-ticket buyers, and how to start showing up there in a way that does not embarrass you.

## Get off the marketplaces first

Before LinkedIn, a small detour. If you are currently selling on Upwork, Fiverr, or any freelance marketplace, get off them as soon as you can.

Marketplaces are not bad. They are just designed for a specific buyer. That buyer arrived to compare prices. They are scanning ratings. They are looking for value, where value mostly means cheap. You can sometimes win on a marketplace by being the rare premium option, but that is the exception, not the rule.

The platform's design is fighting you. The kind of buyer who pays your real price is not on Upwork looking for the cheapest option. They are somewhere else, looking for someone they can trust.

Where they actually are is the next section.

> **Tip:** If marketplaces are paying your bills today, do not quit cold turkey. Build the next channel in parallel. Move work over as it comes in. Quitting income before the replacement is in place is a great way to panic and run back to the cheap work.

## Why LinkedIn, of all places

LinkedIn is unfashionable. It looks corporate. The feed is full of bad posts. Every other update is someone "humbled to announce" something. It is easy to dismiss.

The reasons it works anyway:

- **The mission is aligned.** LinkedIn's stated mission is to create economic opportunity for every member of the global workforce. The people who show up are there for work, not entertainment. That is your buyer.
- **The signal-to-creator ratio is good.** Most LinkedIn users do not post. That means the bar to be visible is much lower than it is on Instagram or TikTok. You can be a small voice and still get seen.
- **The buyers are reachable.** Decision-makers actually use LinkedIn. They check their notifications. They click on profiles. They reply to messages, when the message is worth replying to.
- **The platform tells you who is interested.** LinkedIn shows you who viewed your profile. That is intent data most platforms hide. We will come back to this.

The platform is unfashionable, and that is part of the opportunity. The bar is low. The competition is loud but mostly empty. A practitioner with a real point of view stands out fast.

## Buyer intent on LinkedIn is different

Pay attention to who is actually on the platform. Three groups, each with a different reason to be there.

1. **People looking to upskill or change jobs.** They are reading to learn.
2. **People looking to hire help.** They are reading to vet you.
3. **People selling something.** They are reading to copy and to compete.

Group two is your buyer. The good news is that they exist in real volume. The other good news is that group one will become group two eventually. The bad news is that group three is loud, and most of what you see on the feed comes from them.

Your job is to write for the buyers, not for the other sellers.

> **Warning:** Bots exist on LinkedIn. So does engagement-pod nonsense. But compared to the bigger consumer platforms, LinkedIn is still mostly real people. Do not let a few fakes convince you the whole platform is fake.

## The profile-viewer signal

Here is one of the quietly powerful things about LinkedIn. If you turn on the right settings, you can see who has been viewing your profile.

Most of those views are noise. Some of them are not. When the head of a company you actually want to work with is suddenly looking at your page, that is intent data. Something you said landed. Something they read pulled them.

The move is to reach out, but lightly. Not "I noticed you viewed me, want to buy?" That is creepy. Something closer to: "Saw you stopped by. Anything I can help with?" Short, generous, no pitch.

**Example.** A few years ago, a creator I follow had a viewer from a major game company show up on his profile. He sent a one-line message. That turned into a coffee. The coffee turned into a meeting in another city. The meeting, four months later, turned into a project worth more than a year of his old freelance work.

The point is not that LinkedIn is magic. The point is that intent signals exist, in plain sight, and most people ignore them.

## The two-channel combination that actually works

If you only have time for two channels, the combination that holds up for high-ticket creative work is LinkedIn and YouTube.

- **LinkedIn** is where the conversation happens. Short text, comments, replies, real-time engagement with buyers in your niche.
- **YouTube** is where the proof lives. Longer-form video where the buyer can see how you think, how you talk, how you treat people. It is your modern portfolio.

A buyer reads your LinkedIn post, gets curious, clicks your profile, finds your YouTube. Watches one video. Now they know what you sound like for thirty minutes. The trust this builds is hard to replicate any other way.

You do not need to start both at once. Pick LinkedIn first if you can write. Pick YouTube first if you can talk. Both compound over time.

> **Tip:** "Compound" means a video you make today still earns you views and DMs two years from now. A cold email you send today is dead by Friday. Channels that compound beat channels that do not.

## Try This

Spend thirty minutes on this:

1. Open your LinkedIn profile. Read your headline as if you were a buyer who has never heard of you. Does it tell them what you do, for whom, and what they get? If not, rewrite it. One line.
2. Turn on profile-viewer visibility in your settings.
3. Look at your last five posts. Mark which group they were written for: upskillers, buyers, or other sellers. If most are written for other sellers, your audience problem is partly self-inflicted.
4. Write one short post, today, aimed only at buyers in your niche. Not advice for sellers. Not a quote. Just one observation about a problem your buyer is having this week.

The post does not have to go viral. It has to start telling LinkedIn who your audience is. The algorithm follows the pattern.

## Summary

Cold outreach scales effort, not trust. Marketplaces optimize for the wrong buyer. LinkedIn, as unfashionable as it looks, gives you a platform full of buyers who already came to do business, with a lower creator density and visible intent signals. Pair it with YouTube if you can. Write for the buyer, not the other sellers. The work compounds.
