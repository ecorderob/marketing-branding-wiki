## Why "better" is invisible

Most professionals try to win by being better. Better quality. Better service. More experience. More credentials.

The problem is that "better" is invisible until you have already worked with someone. From the outside, almost every designer, consultant, coach, and agency claims to be better than the alternatives. Buyers cannot tell who is telling the truth. The signal is too noisy. They default to the cheapest option, the one a friend recommended, or the one with the most followers.

What does cut through the noise is being meaningfully different. Different is visible from the outside. Different gives buyers a reason to remember you. Different gives them a story to tell when recommending you to someone else.

This page is about how to be different on purpose, in a way that is grounded in who you actually are.

## The "different is better than better" idea

The phrase has been around in marketing for a long time, popularized through people like Sally Hogshead, and it holds up. In a sea of similar-looking professionals, the only thing that rises above the noise is what stands out.

Modern buying is rarely about features. It is about identity. People do not just buy a product or a service. They buy what working with you says about them. The unspoken question behind most decisions is "who am I, if I work with this person?"

That question is answered by what is different about you, not by what is incrementally better.

This is good news. You do not have to be the world's best at your craft to win. You have to be unmistakably yourself, and that self has to be useful to a specific kind of buyer.

> **Tip:** A useful test. Take your last website, profile, or pitch and remove your name. If a friend reading it can guess it is yours within thirty seconds, the differentiation is working. If they cannot, you are blending in.

## Find the prototype, then break from it

A practical move that works almost every time.

Identify the prototype of someone in your field. The default look, sound, and behavior of professionals at your level. The standard portfolio structure. The standard tone in posts. The standard way of describing the work. The standard photo. The standard color palette. Do not judge it. Just notice what it is.

Then make a list of what everyone in your field does. Call it the ban list. It is the things you will not do, on purpose, because doing them puts you inside the prototype.

You do not need to break every item on the list. One or two clear breaks are enough. Most people in a field cluster tightly together. It only takes one degree of separation to look distinct.

**Example.** Most freelance designers post case studies that focus on the visual outcome. A designer who consistently writes case studies about the business decisions behind the work, with numbers and client conversations included, looks meaningfully different from her peers. She did not need to be a better designer than they are. She just had to break from the prototype on one specific dimension.

> **Warning:** Do not break from the prototype just to be contrary. Break in a direction that is honestly you and that serves the audience you want. Random difference is just noise. Intentional difference is positioning.

## Lean into what is "too much" about you

Most professionals quietly hide the parts of themselves that other people have called "too much."

Too obsessive. Too direct. Too detail-oriented. Too slow. Too intense. Too quiet. Too playful. Too serious. Too whatever.

Those traits, the ones you have been mildly apologizing for, are usually exactly the parts that would make you distinct if you stopped hiding them. The people who built strong personal brands almost always did it by leaning into traits that earlier in their careers they would have edited out.

This does not mean every flaw is a hidden superpower. Some traits really are problems. The ones worth leaning into are the ones that, when expressed, make a specific kind of audience feel "finally, someone who works the way I want to work." If that audience exists, the trait is a signal, not a flaw.

> **Tip:** Ask three people who know you well what makes you different in a slightly inconvenient way. Their answers usually point at something real. The discomfort in the answer is the signal you are close to a real differentiator.

## Be the correct kind of contrarian

A common mistake when people try to be different is to become reflexively contrarian. Whatever the field believes, they argue against. This produces noise without authority.

Real contrarian positioning has a higher bar. Three things have to be true at once.

The first is that you are saying something genuinely different from what most people in your field are saying.

The second is that you are right, or at least you have real evidence and reasoning behind your position. The job is not to be the loudest dissenter. The job is to be a dissenter who turned out to have a point.

The third is that the position is grounded in expertise and experience, not just in a desire for attention. People can usually tell the difference between a contrarian who has thought about something deeply and one who is performing.

Done well, this kind of position is sticky. People remember the take. They remember who said it. They send it to friends. The brand compounds.

**Example.** A pricing consultant whose clear position is "discounting destroys long-term margin, do not do it under any circumstances" is taking a position that is genuinely different from the default ("everyone discounts a little"). It is grounded in numbers and case studies. It produces strong reactions. People who agree become loyal. People who disagree at least remember who said it. That is how a contrarian position works for a brand.

## Drawing a line in the sand

A bolder version of differentiation is naming what you stand against, not just what you stand for.

This is uncomfortable. It is also one of the cleanest ways to attract a tribe. People do not just self-identify with what something is. They self-identify with what something is against.

Famous example: the Dunkin' campaign that ran lines like "Friends don't let friends drink Starbucks." That is a clear line. Dunkin' is for one kind of coffee drinker. Starbucks is for another. There is no fence. You self-select into one camp.

You do not have to be that aggressive. You can draw quieter lines that still do the work. "I do not work with clients who treat my team poorly." "I do not run unpaid pitches." "I do not redesign things for the sake of redesigning them." "I do not work in industries that I think are net-harmful."

Each line filters out one kind of relationship and attracts another. The professionals who feel most powerfully positioned are usually the ones who are clear about both halves: what they are for and what they are not.

> **Definition:** Line in the sand. A public position about what you stand against, not just what you stand for. Used carefully, it strengthens identification with the buyers who agree and filters out the ones who would have been a bad fit anyway.

## Different by design, not by accident

A few practical sources of intentional differentiation that work in many fields.

- **A specific audience.** Most professionals describe their target client too broadly. Choosing a more specific audience is differentiation by itself.
- **A specific method.** Naming the way you work, even if it is not radically new, gives buyers something to remember. "The two-week brand sprint." "The audit-first model." "The discovery-only first engagement."
- **A specific worldview.** A clear point of view about what good work looks like and why. People follow worldviews more readily than they follow tactics.
- **A specific format or rhythm.** Long-form essays. Weekly notes. A particular cadence of public work that becomes recognizable over time.

You do not need all four. One that you commit to and live with for years is more powerful than four that you experiment with for a quarter each.

## Try this

Spend twenty minutes on three short exercises.

First, write down five things that are typical of professionals at your level in your field. The prototype. Be honest. The way they sound, the way their portfolios are structured, the way they describe their work, the topics they talk about, the format they use.

Second, mark the items on the list that you have unconsciously copied because they felt safe. Pick one to break from on purpose, in a direction that feels more like you.

Third, write one sentence describing something you are clearly against in your field. A practice you will not adopt. A type of project you will not take. A common piece of advice you do not believe. Read the sentence twice. If it makes you slightly uncomfortable to publish it, it is probably the right one.

## Summary

Better is invisible from the outside. Different is what cuts through the noise. Find the prototype in your field and break from it on one or two specific dimensions, in a direction that is honestly you. Lean into the traits other people have called "too much" about you, especially the ones that attract a specific kind of audience. Use contrarian positioning carefully. The bar is genuinely different, supported by evidence, and grounded in real expertise. Draw quiet or loud lines about what you stand against, not just what you stand for. Different by design beats different by accident. The body of work that emerges is recognizable, defendable, and your own.
