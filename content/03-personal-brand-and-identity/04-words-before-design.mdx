## Logo first is the wrong order

Walk into most personal-brand projects in their early stages and you will find someone agonizing over a color palette before they have written a single sentence about what their work is for.

That is the wrong order. It is like designing a beautiful storefront before deciding what the shop will sell. The window display might be lovely. It will not help you.

The fix is simple. Get the words right first. Once the words are right, every design decision later in the process becomes much easier, because you will have a clear idea of what the design is supposed to express.

This page is the language work that has to come before any visual design. It is also useful even if you never design anything yourself, because every email, post, and conversation runs through the same words.

## Why words come first

Visual design is downstream of meaning. Colors, typefaces, photography, layout, and imagery all serve a message. If the message is unclear, the design has nothing to serve, and the work ends up looking competent but empty.

The professionals who build distinctive personal brands almost always have language work that came before any visual identity. They knew what they sounded like in words before they decided what they looked like in pixels. The visual then expressed the language, not the other way around.

A practical consequence: if you work on the words first, you can also test the brand without touching design tools at all. You can write a post in your brand voice today. You can answer a client email in your brand voice tomorrow. The visual identity, when it eventually arrives, will be a layer on top of an already working personality.

> **Definition:** Words-first branding. The practice of articulating your brand's voice, personality, and core message in language before making any visual design choices. The language becomes the brief that the design then serves.

## Three things to write down before any design

Three pieces of language carry most of the weight.

The first is your **brand personality** in adjectives. Three words is enough. Not generic words like "professional" or "creative." Specific ones. "Calm, precise, slightly dry." "Warm, blunt, curious." "Sharp, irreverent, useful." The specifics matter because the goal is to differentiate, not to fit into a template.

The second is your **voice and tone**. How does this brand sound when it talks? Long sentences or short? Formal or conversational? Do you make jokes? Do you swear? Do you ask questions or make claims? Write a few example sentences and read them out loud. The voice is whatever sounds right and stays consistent.

The third is your **brand essence**. One paragraph. Not a tagline, but a clear statement of what you are about and what you do for who. Write it as if explaining your work to a curious friend.

When these three things are written and you are happy with them, you have a brief. Every later design choice can be tested against the brief. Does this color palette feel calm, precise, and slightly dry? Does this typeface sound like the voice we wrote? Does the homepage say what the essence paragraph says, in fewer words?

> **Tip:** Spend more time on the adjectives than feels reasonable. Most people pick adjectives in five minutes and then live with them for years. A better approach is to live with three or four candidate adjectives for two weeks, write in each, and notice which set sounds least like a costume.

## Use precise language, not jargon

A common mistake when writing brand language is reaching for vocabulary that sounds elevated but is actually vague.

Words like "innovative," "dynamic," "leading," "passionate," "world-class," and "synergistic" are all empty in this context. Almost every brand says them. None of them describe anything specific.

The way to find better words is to use a thesaurus, but for a different reason than most people use one. You are not looking for a fancier version of "creative." You are looking for the specific word that captures something about how you actually work.

"Precise" is more useful than "high-quality." "Dry" is more useful than "professional." "Blunt" is more useful than "direct." "Slow" is more useful than "considered." Each of those words gives a designer, a writer, or a future you something concrete to work with.

> **Warning:** If two of your three brand adjectives could appear on a competitor's site without changing anything, you have not gone specific enough. Try again.

## What this looks like in practice

A few real examples of language driving design.

Pinkberry famously used the line "Swirling daily" instead of "Open 9 to 5." Same information. The first one carries the playful, soft personality the brand wanted. A more corporate phrasing would have been an immediate mismatch with their visual identity.

Mailchimp built one of the more recognizable voices in software by being consistently quirky, encouraging, and unconventional across every channel. Error messages, marketing emails, support replies, and onboarding flows all sound like the same person. That voice came from a written brief that the design team and the writers shared. The visuals follow.

You do not have to be a software company to do this. A solo consultant can pick three adjectives and a one-paragraph essence statement, and run every email and post through that filter. The discipline is the same.

**Example.** A designer's brief, written before any visual work:

- Personality: calm, precise, slightly dry.
- Voice: short sentences. Few exclamation points. Specific examples over abstract claims. Comfortable saying "I do not know yet."
- Essence: I help small SaaS teams turn confusing products into clear ones. I do not redesign for the sake of redesigning. I redesign when the current design is the reason something is not working.

That is a brief. It is also enough to write a website with no logo, post on LinkedIn for a year, and brief any future designer in five minutes.

## The everyday filter

Once the brief exists, it becomes a filter you run on small things, not just on big design projects.

Every email, every post, every meeting opener, every proposal can be checked against the brief. Does this sentence sound calm and precise? Does this slightly-dry voice come through, or did I default to a more polished, generic register?

Over time, this filter is what builds the gut feeling people develop about you. It is not the logo. It is the consistency of how you sound, in every channel, over years.

The good news is that this kind of consistency is much easier to maintain than visual consistency. You do not need a brand book to remember three adjectives and one essence paragraph. You need them written somewhere you can re-read once a week until they become automatic.

## Try this

Open a notes file. Title it "Brand brief, version 1."

Write three adjectives that describe how you want your work to feel to other people. Spend ten minutes. Reject anything that sounds generic.

Write five example sentences in the voice that matches those adjectives. Read them out loud. If they sound like a friend describing their work, keep them. If they sound like a press release, rewrite them.

Write one short paragraph that captures the essence: who you help, what you help them with, and the way you do it. Six sentences max.

Save the file. Re-read it before writing your next email or post. You will catch yourself drifting almost immediately. The filter is the work.

## Summary

Visual identity is downstream of language. Get the words right first, and every design choice later becomes obvious. Three pieces of language carry most of the weight: three specific personality adjectives, a written voice and tone, and a one-paragraph essence statement. Avoid vague vocabulary that sounds elevated but means nothing. Use specific words that give future-you and any designer something concrete to work with. Once the brief exists, run every email, post, and proposal through it. The consistency of how you sound, over years, is what builds the gut feeling people carry around about you.
