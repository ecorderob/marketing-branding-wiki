## The three stories you need ready

When someone asks "what do you do?" most professionals answer with a job title. That is not enough.

People remember stories. They do not remember job titles. The professionals who land in your head and stay there usually have three short stories ready, even if they have never named them. The story of where they came from. The story of the moment that changed their direction. The story of what they finally decided to be known for.

This page gives you the structure to find your own three. You will not invent them. You already lived them. The work is to recognize them and put them into words.

## Why three, and why these three

Each story does a different job.

The first one, the Origin Story, explains where your point of view came from. It tells people why you see the world the way you do. It is not your resume. It is the experience that gave you your particular lens.

The second one, the Inciting Incident, explains the moment your path bent. The event, the failure, the realization, the small moment that nudged you off the default track and onto the one you are on now.

The third one, the Defining Moment, explains the choice you eventually made about what you wanted to be known for. The point at which you stopped being a generalist and started leaning into one specific thing.

Together, the three answer the question that lives behind every "what do you do?" The real question is "why are you the person to do this?" The three stories answer it.

## Story 1: Origin

The Origin Story is the backdrop, not the timeline.

It is not "I went to art school in 2008, then worked at three agencies, then went freelance in 2015." That is a job history. Job histories are forgettable.

The Origin Story is the formative experience that shaped how you see your work. It usually starts much earlier than your first job and includes details from the part of your life that had nothing to do with the work you eventually chose.

A useful sentence template:

> "I grew up in [circumstances] where [key experience] taught me [core value or lens]. That still shapes how I [current professional approach]."

**Example.** James Dyson's Origin Story is not "I am a British engineer." It is closer to "I get genuinely irritated by things that do not work well, and I cannot stop iterating on them until they do." That is a lens, not a credential. It shapes everything else he does and explains why a single product took five thousand prototypes.

**Example.** A designer who moved between five countries as a child says, "I learned early that environments shift, and you adapt or you suffer. I bring that adaptability to client projects. I do not panic when the brief changes."

Notice that neither example is dramatic. They do not need to be. Authentic small details land better than forced big ones. The reader should feel "ah, that explains why this person sees their work the way they do."

> **Tip:** Reflection prompts that surface a real Origin Story. What formative experience gave you your particular lens? Who or what built your taste? What early difficulty built a specific muscle that shows up in your work today? What about your background gives you a perspective most people in your field do not have?

## Story 2: Inciting Incident

The Inciting Incident is the moment your trajectory changed.

It is usually one moment, not a slow drift. A specific event. A failure. A realization. A piece of feedback that landed harder than you expected. A project that went unexpectedly right or unexpectedly wrong. A conversation with someone that flipped a switch.

The structure that works:

> "Everything changed when [event or realization]. I realized [insight] and decided [pivotal choice]. That taught me [lesson] which now drives [current approach]."

**Example.** Howard Schultz did not slowly become the founder of modern Starbucks. There was a specific moment in Milan, sitting in an espresso bar, where he understood that coffee in Italy was an experience, not just a beverage. The trip changed his entire idea of what a coffee company could be. The story has a clear before and after.

**Example.** A brand strategist's Inciting Incident: "I had spent three years executing other people's visions. Then a small nonprofit asked me to help figure out who they were before deciding what to make. That single project taught me that strategy comes before execution. I have not gone back."

The key is that the listener can identify the line. There was a way you saw the world before this happened, and a different way after. Both are nameable.

> **Warning:** Resist the urge to make the story bigger than it was. The Inciting Incident does not have to be dramatic. It has to be specific. A small moment, told precisely, lands harder than an inflated one.

## Story 3: Defining Moment

The Defining Moment is when you consciously chose what to be known for.

It is the moment your unique strength clicked into place. Or the moment you decided to specialize. Or the moment you realized you wanted a specific kind of impact, on a specific kind of person, with a specific kind of work.

The structure:

> "I discovered my calling when [recognition]. I realized I was naturally [strength] and could help [specific people] with [specific value]. That became the foundation for [current focus]."

**Example.** Yvon Chouinard's Defining Moment for Patagonia is when he decided the company would prioritize environmental impact even when it cost them sales. The "Don't Buy This Jacket" advertising in 2011, asking customers not to buy something they did not really need, was a public expression of a choice that had already been made internally. That choice became the brand.

**Example.** A coach: "I realized my real gift was not in having all the answers. It was in asking the question that helped someone find their own. Once I named that, my whole offer changed."

Two markers tell you have a real Defining Moment. First, you can name what makes you distinctly valuable, in a sentence. Second, you can name the kind of person you most want to help, in a sentence.

## How to find your three

If the stories are not obvious to you yet, that is normal. Most people have not put them into words.

A few prompts that surface them faster.

For the Origin Story, ask yourself what about your background gives you a lens that most people in your field do not have. The lens is the story. The job history is not.

For the Inciting Incident, ask yourself what specific moment you can point to where your direction shifted. If you cannot name a moment, look for a project, a conversation, or a piece of feedback. Most people remember at least one, even if they have never told the story out loud.

For the Defining Moment, ask yourself when you stopped trying to be everything to everyone and chose one specific thing to be known for. If you have not made that choice yet, the Defining Moment is in the future, and the work of category 04, on specialization, is what produces it.

> **Tip:** The fastest way to find any of the three is to tell the story out loud to a friend, then ask them which part they remember a week later. The part they remember is the real story. The rest is connective tissue you can cut.

## Stories versus pitches

A note on tone.

The three stories are not elevator pitches. They are not designed to sell. They do their work because they are real.

If your Origin Story sounds like a marketing line, rewrite it in plain language. If your Inciting Incident sounds like a Hollywood beat, take out the drama. If your Defining Moment sounds like positioning copy, sit with the actual moment instead.

The audience does not need a pitch. They need to understand who they are talking to. The three stories give them that, in fewer words than a pitch and with more weight.

## Try this

Open a notes file with three sections, labeled Origin, Inciting Incident, Defining Moment.

Spend twenty minutes on each section. Just write. No structure. No editing. Get the rough version of each story onto the page.

Then, on a second pass, write each story as a single short paragraph using the three sentence templates from this page. Read all three out loud, in order.

If they hold together as a single arc that explains why you do what you do, you have your three. They will keep evolving. The first version is enough.

## Summary

A strong personal brand has three short stories underneath it. The Origin Story explains the lens you bring, shaped by something earlier than your first job. The Inciting Incident names the specific moment that bent your trajectory. The Defining Moment names the choice you made about what to be known for and who to help. Each story is short, real, and told in your own words. Together, they answer the question every prospect quietly asks: why is this the person for this work?
