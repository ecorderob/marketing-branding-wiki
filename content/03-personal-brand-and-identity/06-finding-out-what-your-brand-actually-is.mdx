## You do not get to decide

Here is the part of personal branding that most people skip, because it is uncomfortable.

Your brand is not what you think it is. It is what other people think it is. The gut feeling lives in their heads. You can shape the inputs over time, but on any given day, the brand you have is not the brand you intended. It is the brand you are actually transmitting.

Before you redesign anything, you need a real picture of the brand you have right now. Otherwise you will redesign for a problem that is not the actual problem.

This page is three short exercises that surface the brand you actually have. They are uncomfortable. They are also the cheapest way to see your own positioning honestly.

## Exercise 1: the three-words mirror

The first exercise is the simplest and the most useful.

Pick five trusted contacts from different parts of your professional life. A current client. A past client. A peer. A collaborator. A former colleague.

Ask each of them the same question. Do not coach. Do not give context. Just ask.

> "If you had to describe me professionally in three words, what comes to mind first?"

Wait for the answer. Do not react. Write down what they say.

When you have all five sets of three words, lay them out on a page. Look for the overlaps. The recurring words, especially the ones that show up across very different relationships, are your brand as it currently lives in others' minds.

Two outcomes are common.

The first outcome is that the recurring words match what you hoped people would say. That is a sign your transmission is working.

The second outcome is more useful. The recurring words are not what you expected. Maybe everyone called you "reliable" and "calm" when you have been trying to position yourself as "bold" and "creative." Maybe everyone called you "thorough" when you wanted to be seen as "fast." That gap is the gap you actually have to close.

> **Tip:** Resist the urge to argue with the answers. The five people are not telling you who you are. They are telling you what your behavior has signaled to them. The behavior is the brand.

## Exercise 2: the work magnet

The second exercise looks at what kind of work finds its way to you.

Take your last five to ten projects. Forget how you marketed yourself. Look at what people actually came to you for. Write a short label for each project that describes the real problem you solved, not the deliverable.

A few questions to ask of the list.

- What kind of project keeps showing up?
- What kind of client keeps finding you?
- What problem do you keep being hired to solve?
- What is the project type that pays best, and how often does it appear?
- What do people quietly assume you are brilliant at, even when you have not claimed it?

The pattern is your brand in the market, not in your bio.

If you market yourself as a strategist but every project is execution, the market thinks you are an executor. If you market yourself as a logo designer but most of the inbound is brand strategy work, the market thinks you are a strategist. The market is more honest than the website.

The work magnet is also useful when you decide to change your positioning. You can either lean into what the market is already telling you, which is usually faster, or you can decide to fight the current and reposition on purpose, which is slower but may be necessary if the work that finds you is not the work you want.

**Example.** A designer kept getting hired by anxious founders for projects that other agencies had bailed on. Her stated positioning was "brand identity for tech startups." Her actual brand, surfaced by the work magnet, was "the designer who rescues messy projects." Once she rewrote her positioning to match what was already happening, the same projects that found her by accident started finding her on purpose, and she was able to charge more for them.

## Exercise 3: the collective impression

The third exercise is a thought experiment, not a research project.

Imagine you could gather one hundred people who know you. Family, friends, colleagues, current and past clients, collaborators, peers. Imagine that you could strip out the two outliers on each end. The two who think you are unusually amazing. The two who think you are unusually difficult.

Look at what the middle ninety-six people would say.

That residual impression is closer to your brand than either your self-perception or the loudest piece of feedback you ever received. The fans and the critics both produce noise. The middle is the signal.

This exercise works because it forces you to think in distributions. Most people, when they think about how they are perceived, anchor on the loudest data point. The client who praised them most. The colleague who disliked them. The post that went well. The post that flopped. None of those define the brand. The middle does.

> **Definition:** Collective impression. The residual gut feeling that the people who know you would converge on if you removed the outliers. A more accurate picture of your current brand than any single piece of feedback.

## A small note on the difference between brand and trauma

A real warning about doing these exercises.

The three words you collect, the work magnet patterns, and the imagined consensus will sometimes hand you a piece of feedback that stings. Especially the three words.

Do not pretend it does not. Also do not let it run the show.

The right reaction is to treat the data the way you would treat a useful client interview. Notice it. Write it down. Sit with it for a few days before you decide what to do with it. Some of the unflattering signal will be accurate and worth changing. Some of it will be a snapshot of a specific relationship that does not reflect the broader pattern.

The professionals who come out the other side of these exercises stronger are the ones who can hold the feedback without either rejecting it or being crushed by it. The ones who reject it stay where they were. The ones who collapse under it overcorrect into a new persona that is just as inaccurate as the old one.

> **Warning:** A single piece of harsh feedback is not a brand audit. The pattern across multiple sources is. Run all three exercises before deciding what to change.

## What to do with what you learn

The three exercises usually surface one of three patterns.

**Pattern one: aligned.** The three words match your intended brand. The work magnet matches your stated positioning. The collective impression is more or less what you hoped. In this case, the work is to keep going, with more consistency. You are transmitting clearly.

**Pattern two: lagging.** Your behavior has shifted, but the impressions have not caught up. People still think of you as the version of you from two years ago. In this case, the work is patience and visibility. The brand is on a delay. Keep showing up consistently and the impressions will update.

**Pattern three: misaligned.** What you intend and what people perceive are different in a way that is not just a delay. The market is telling you something true that your self-image is not seeing. In this case, the work is harder. You either lean into what is already true and make it the brand on purpose, or you start a slower campaign of behavior change to shift what people experience.

There is no right answer between the third options. The only wrong answer is to pretend the gap does not exist.

## Try this

Pick one of the three exercises. Run it this week.

If you have never asked anyone the three-words question, start there. Five contacts. One question each. No follow-up explaining what you meant. Twenty minutes of work, plus the time to collect responses.

Lay out what you receive. Look for the overlap. Write a single sentence describing your actual brand as the data shows it. Compare it to a single sentence describing your intended brand.

If those two sentences match, keep doing what you are doing. If they do not, the gap between them is the most useful piece of information you will get all month.

## Summary

Your brand is not what you intend. It is what other people perceive. Three small exercises surface the actual brand you have. The three-words mirror reveals what trusted contacts associate with your name. The work magnet shows what kind of projects and problems find you regardless of your stated positioning. The collective impression imagines the residual consensus across everyone who knows you. The most common outcomes are aligned, lagging, or misaligned, and each one calls for a different response. Run at least one of these exercises before deciding to redesign anything visible. The data is uncomfortable and it is also the cheapest, fastest way to see your real positioning.
