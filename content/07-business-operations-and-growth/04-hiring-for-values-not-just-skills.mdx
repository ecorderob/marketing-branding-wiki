## Most bad hires were not skill problems

Look at the worst hire you ever made. Run through the story.

Almost always, the issue was not that they could not do the job. They could. The issue was how they handled feedback. Or how they treated junior people. Or what they did when something went wrong. The friction was about values, not skills.

Most hiring processes get this exactly backwards. They filter on skills first and check values last, if at all. This page flips that order, with two filters that hold up over time.

By the end you will be able to:

- Run a hiring process where values come before cultural fit, and cultural fit comes before skills.
- Apply the 5X Rule before you write a single job description.
- Ask reference-check questions that surface the things skill checks miss.

## The values-first sequence

> **Definition:** Values-first hiring. A hiring process where you filter candidates in three sequential steps. Values alignment first, cultural fit second, skills last. Skills can be developed. Values misalignment cannot.

Three steps. Strict order.

### Step 1. Values alignment

Before you ask anything technical, find out how this person operates when nobody is watching.

The questions look like:

- "Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone in authority. What did you do?"
- "Describe a moment when you chose a slower path because it was the right one."
- "When was the last time you had to give someone news they did not want to hear?"
- "Walk me through a time you made a real mistake. What happened?"
- "What is something you stopped doing in the last two years because it stopped feeling right?"

The point is not the answer. The point is whether the answer is specific. Vague answers usually mean the candidate has not lived the value they are claiming. Specific answers, with names, dates, and small details, usually mean they have.

### Step 2. Cultural fit

Once values pass, look at how this person fits the team.

You are checking:

- How they treat people with less authority. Look at how they speak to a junior team member, an admin, an intern. The tone there is the real one.
- How they ask questions. Curious questions are healthy. Quizzing-the-interviewer questions are a flag.
- How they handle silence. People who fill every pause with talking usually fill every project meeting with talking too.

Most hiring processes skip this. Or they outsource it to a "team interview" that turns into another technical screen. That is a missed opportunity. The team interview is where the cultural fit lives.

### Step 3. Skills

Now, and only now, do you check whether they can do the job.

If a candidate passes step one and step two but is at eighty percent of the technical bar, hire them. They will catch up. The reverse, hiring a strong technical fit who fails on values, is the move that creates regret.

> **Warning:** Reversing the order does not make the process faster. It makes it cheaper to hire and more expensive to fire. The most expensive employees in any business are not the highest-paid. They are the ones whose values do not match.

## The 5X Rule

Before you post the job, run the math.

> **Definition:** The 5X Rule. Every team member should generate roughly five times their total compensation in value, where compensation includes salary, benefits, taxes, equipment, software, and overhead.

The math is unkind on purpose.

A 60,000 employee:

- Salary: 60,000.
- Plus benefits, taxes, equipment, software, training, overhead share. Total real cost is closer to 80,000 to 90,000.
- The role needs to contribute toward roughly 300,000 in revenue or value saved.

If you cannot draw the path from this hire to that contribution, you are not ready to hire. No matter how overwhelmed you feel.

This is not about exploitation. It is about the structural reality of small businesses. A new hire starts as a cost on day one. They become an investment somewhere between month three and month nine. If the math from the start does not show a clear path to 5X, the role will quietly drag on margins for as long as the person is there.

> **Tip:** If you cannot pass the 5X Rule for a given role, do not give up on the hire. Consider whether a contractor, a fractional, or an automation gets you to the same outcome at lower fixed cost. Many "we need to hire" moments are really "we need a system" moments.

## Reference checks for values, not skills

The reference check is the most under-used step in most hiring processes. It is treated as a formality. Most reference questions confirm what the resume already said.

The questions that actually find things:

- "How does this person handle critical feedback?"
- "What motivates them most? What drains them?"
- "How do they respond when things go wrong?"
- "How do they treat people with less authority than them?"
- "What would make you hesitate to hire them again?"

The last one is the most useful, and the most uncomfortable to ask. A good reference will pause before answering it. The pause is the data. The thing they say after the pause is even more data.

You are listening for two things. Patterns. Hesitations. Patterns tell you what is consistent. Hesitations tell you what the reference does not want to put in writing.

## The core values worksheet

Before any of this works, you need to have written down what your own values actually are. Not the corporate ones from the website. The real ones.

A short exercise:

### Step 1. Long list

Brainstorm fifteen to twenty values that resonate. Quality, integrity, generosity, transparency, craftsmanship, boldness, growth, independence, calm, directness, kindness. Pick whatever shows up. Do not edit.

### Step 2. Peak experiences

Identify three peak career moments. Times when work felt right. For each, write down which values were honored.

### Step 3. Frustrating experiences

Identify three frustrating career moments. Times when work felt wrong. For each, write down which values were violated.

### Step 4. Refine

The values that show up in both step two and step three are usually the real ones. Narrow to five or seven. For each, write a behavioral translation:

- In client relationships, this value looks like ...
- In team interactions, this value looks like ...
- In business decisions, this value looks like ...

The test is honest. Do you actually behave this way, or do you wish you did? If "wish," it is aspirational. Move it off the list. Aspirational values create the worst kind of friction. The team can tell when the leader is not living what they posted.

> **Warning:** Five real values beat ten beautiful ones. If you cannot point to specific behaviors you do every week that embody a value, the value is not yet yours.

## Use AI to pressure-test the role before you post it

Before you write the job description, run the role through a stress-test prompt.

```text
You are a senior operations and HR advisor for small creative businesses. I am about to hire for the following role:

- Title: ...
- Total cost (salary + benefits + taxes + equipment + overhead): ...
- Tasks the role would handle: ...
- The 3 outcomes I expect them to drive in year one: ...
- My top 5 values: ...

Do this:
1. Apply the 5X Rule. Show the math.
2. Tell me whether the listed tasks justify a hire or whether a contractor, fractional, or automation would deliver the same outcome at lower fixed cost.
3. Suggest 5 values-based interview questions tailored to my values list.
4. Suggest 5 reference-check questions that probe values rather than skills.
5. Flag the single biggest risk you see in this hire.

Be direct. If the role does not pass the 5X Rule, say so plainly.
```

Read the answer. Treat the math as a sanity check, not a verdict. The decision is yours.

## Try This

One sitting. Forty-five minutes:

1. Take any role you are considering hiring for.
2. Run the 5X Rule on paper. If the math fails, stop and consider whether a contractor or an automation solves it.
3. Write five values-based interview questions, in your own words, tied to your real values.
4. Write five reference-check questions from the list above, plus one of your own.
5. Show all of it to one person you trust. Ask them which question they would change.

The shift from "hiring is hard" to "hiring is structured" usually happens around the second or third role you run through this process.

## Summary

Hire for values, then cultural fit, then skills. Skills can be taught. Values cannot. The 5X Rule keeps you honest about whether a role can pay for itself. Reference checks for values, not skills, surface the things every other interview misses. None of this prevents bad hires entirely. It just changes the ratio. Most of the worst hires you have already made would have been caught by the values question you did not ask.
