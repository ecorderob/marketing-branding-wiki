## Diversification done badly

Most creative owners diversify by accident. A friend asks them to speak. A client requests a course. A platform offers a sponsorship. They say yes to everything, end up with five half-finished income streams, and burn out without any of them paying off.

There is a better order. The streams that work compound on each other. Each one earns the right to exist by being built on the one before it. Skip steps and the whole thing wobbles.

This page is a four-rung ladder. The order matters more than the steps themselves.

## The ladder, in order

> **Definition:** The Income Diversification Ladder. Four sequential rungs of income, where each rung is built on the credibility, audience, and systems established by the one below it. Rung 1: master your core service at premium rates. Rung 2: package expertise into products or courses. Rung 3: monetize your platform. Rung 4: speaking, licensing, partnerships.

### Rung 1. Master your core service at premium rates

Before anything else, become known for one thing.

This means:

- Premium pricing that reflects real value, not market average.
- A repeatable process that delivers consistent results.
- A handful of strong case studies you can point at.
- A referral pipeline that brings in at least some inbound work.

If any of these is missing, you are not on rung 1 yet. You are still building it. Adding a course or a paid newsletter on top of a wobbly rung 1 is what causes most "diversification" to fail.

The signal that you are ready to climb is calm. You have inbound. Your pricing holds. Your process is documented enough that someone else could follow it.

> **Warning:** A common trap is to skip rung 1 because the core service feels boring. The boredom is the asset. It means the work is repeatable. Repeatable work funds everything above it. Until rung 1 is calm and predictable, the rungs above are distractions, not opportunities.

### Rung 2. Package your expertise into products or courses

Once rung 1 is stable, you have something to package. Not the entire service. A piece of it.

The mistake here is going too big. Most first courses are six-week, twelve-module monsters that take nine months to build, three months to launch, and end up generating less than the time would have made on rung 1.

Use a Minimum Viable Course approach instead.

> **Definition:** Minimum Viable Course. The smallest version of your course that solves one specific problem for one specific person. Often four to six hours of content, priced under 200, used to validate demand before any larger investment.

The MVC tests one thing. Will the right person pay any money for the smallest possible version of this knowledge?

If yes, you have a signal. Now you can build a longer version, a higher price tier, a community layer. If no, you have saved yourself nine months. Either outcome is a win.

A good rung 2 product:

- Targets a single specific skill.
- Promises a measurable outcome.
- Stays small. Four to six hours of material is plenty for the first version.
- Is priced for traction, not for prestige. Under 200 is fine to start.
- Gets feedback you can use to iterate.

### Rung 3. Monetize your platform

By now you have an audience. Some of them have bought from you. Most have not but read or watch you regularly.

Now you can layer in:

- Affiliate partnerships for tools you actually use. Not for the highest commission. For the tools you would recommend anyway.
- Sponsorships from companies serving your audience. Vet hard. The audience trusts you because you have not sold them out yet.
- Premium content, memberships, or knowledge hubs. Recurring revenue from people who want a deeper relationship with you.

Rung 3 is where revenue starts decoupling from your hours. That is the point. The work is built on the trust you earned in rungs 1 and 2.

> **Tip:** The rule for affiliates and sponsorships is simple. If you would not recommend it without the money, do not recommend it with the money. The trust is the asset. One sleazy promotion costs you more than the deal pays.

### Rung 4. Speaking, licensing, partnerships

Rung 4 is leverage on everything below it.

- Paid speaking. Your case studies and content earn you stages.
- Licensing. Your frameworks, processes, or curricula get sold to other businesses to use.
- Strategic partnerships with companies that already serve your audience.

This rung is hard to plan. It usually shows up as a side effect of doing the first three rungs well. The work is mostly about being ready to say yes when the right opportunity appears, and to say no when the wrong one does.

## A three-year picture

A reasonable shape for a solo creative practice climbing the ladder:

| Year | Focus | What success looks like |
|----|----|----|
| 1 | Rung 1 only | Core service is calm, premium-priced, with 3 to 5 strong case studies |
| 2 | Rung 1 + first MVC | First course launched, validated, and earning a meaningful share of revenue |
| 3 | Add rung 3 | Audience monetization layer in place, recurring revenue starting |

Rung 4 happens when it happens. Pushing for it before the lower rungs are solid usually backfires.

> **Warning:** "Year" is a placeholder. Some people climb faster. Some slower. The numbers do not matter. The order does. If your year-2 plan starts with rung 3, you have not earned the audience yet to support it.

## The mercenary phase

Sometimes you are not on rung 1 yet, and the work that pays the bills is work you do not love. That is not a moral problem. It is a phase.

> **Definition:** The Mercenary Phase. A deliberate, time-boxed period of taking high-paying work you are not passionate about, in order to build savings and runway that lets you make the next move from a position of strength rather than desperation.

The risk is staying too long. The mercenary phase is funded runway, not a career. Two anchors keep it honest.

- A specific savings target. "When I have X in the bank, I move."
- A specific time horizon. "I will do this for eighteen months at most."

Whichever comes first, you exit. On purpose. With a plan for what is next.

The phase is also useful for what it teaches. You will learn fast what you do not want, what you cannot tolerate, and what kind of business you actually want to build. That clarity is hard to get any other way.

## The 10x vision exercise

When deciding what to build on the ladder, the right question is rarely "how do I grow ten percent next year?" That question produces small tweaks to the existing system. Useful, but limited.

The better question is "what would have to be true for me to be at ten times this in three years?"

A short version of the exercise:

1. Write your current state. Revenue, hours worked, number of clients, team size, project types.
2. Multiply each number by ten. Imagine the new state on paper.
3. List which of your current systems would shatter under that load. Pricing model. Production process. Team size. Sales pipeline.
4. Reverse engineer. What would have to be different for the ten-times version to work?

The point is not to literally hit 10x. The point is to use the question as a mirror. It forces you to see which decisions are in the direction of expansion, and which are just keeping you busy.

> **Tip:** Run the 10x exercise once a quarter. The answers change as you grow. The ones that stay the same are usually pointing at the constraints you have been avoiding.

## Use AI to plan the next rung

When you are deciding what to build next, get a second opinion before you commit a quarter to it.

A useful prompt:

```text
You are a strategic advisor for a creative business. Here is the current state:

- Core service: ...
- Annual revenue: ...
- Audience size on the main platform: ...
- Existing products or courses: ...
- Time available per week to build something new: ...

Apply the Income Diversification Ladder. Tell me:
1. Which rung am I currently on, honestly?
2. Is rung 1 actually calm, or am I tempted to skip it?
3. If I have the bandwidth to build one new thing in the next 90 days, which rung should it be on, and what is the smallest version of that thing?
4. The 3 most likely ways I will sabotage this plan.

Push back. Do not flatter.
```

Use the answer as a sparring partner. The decision is yours.

## Try This

Forty minutes:

1. Honestly mark which rung you are actually on. Not the rung you wish you were on.
2. List three signals that would tell you that rung is solid (calm, repeatable, predictable revenue, strong case studies).
3. For each signal, mark whether it is true today.
4. If any signal is missing, your next 90 days belong to fixing it. Not to climbing.
5. If all signals are true, define the smallest possible version of a rung 2 offering and put a launch date on the calendar.

The hardest part of this exercise is being honest about which rung you are actually on. Most people are one rung lower than they think.

## Summary

Diversification only works in order. Master your core service. Package the expertise. Monetize the platform. Then layer in speaking, licensing, and partnerships. Skipping rungs creates fragmentation that looks like growth and feels like burnout. The Mercenary Phase is a fine way to get to rung 1, as long as it is funded runway, not a career. The 10x vision exercise stops you from optimizing a system that should be replaced. The order is the strategy. Everything else is execution.
