## The number nobody calculates

Most creative owners can tell you their hourly rate. Almost none of them can tell you their actual one.

There is a gap between the rate on the proposal and the rate the math shows. That gap is where profit goes to die. It is silent. It does not show up on the invoice. It shows up in tired Sundays, missed savings, and the strange feeling that you are working harder every year and not getting ahead.

This page is a four-step diagnostic that surfaces the leak and a 90-day plan to close it. Nothing here requires more clients. Just honest math.

## Step 1: Pricing reality check

Start by writing down what you charge today. The real number, not the one on the marketing page.

For each main offer:

- The price you list publicly.
- The price you usually agree to after a small discount.
- The price you actually invoice when scope creep is included.

The third number is the one that matters. For most people, it is fifteen to thirty percent below the first. That alone is a finding.

Most creatives also price by the hour or by some loose feel for the project. Both miss the same thing. Neither is anchored to what your business actually needs to survive. We will get there in step three.

## Step 2: True time accounting

Pick one project from the last quarter. Ideally a recent one. Reconstruct, hour by hour, every minute you spent on it.

Include all of the following:

- Discovery calls and proposal writing.
- Research and reading.
- Internal team coordination.
- Client revisions and email back-and-forth.
- Admin tied to the project (invoicing, reminders, scheduling).
- The cost of context-switching, in actual hours lost between sessions.

You will land on a number that is forty to one hundred percent higher than what you billed for. That is normal. It is also why your year-end profit looks different from the spreadsheet you ran in January.

> **Tip:** Time tracking feels heavy until you do it for one project. Then it pays for itself for the rest of your career. You only need one honest reconstruction to see the pattern in everything else.

## Step 3: Your business lifeline number

This is the number that ends a lot of arguments with yourself.

A simple version of the formula:

```text
Lifeline rate = (annual costs + target profit) / billable hours per year
```

Annual costs include software, tools, taxes, insurance, your salary, savings goals, time off, retirement contribution, sick days, family obligations. The boring stuff. Add it all up.

Target profit is what you want left over after all of that, in real money, not a percentage.

Billable hours are not 2,080 (52 weeks times 40 hours). They are usually closer to 1,000 to 1,200 once you remove vacation, sick days, marketing time, admin, and unbillable internal work. Use the real number.

Divide. The rate that comes out is your floor. Anything below it is a slow loss disguised as a project.

**Example.** Imagine a solo designer.

- Annual costs (incl. owner salary and savings): 120,000.
- Target profit: 30,000.
- Billable hours: 1,000.
- Lifeline rate: 150 per hour.

If they are charging 100 per hour, they are losing 50 per billable hour. Across 1,000 hours, that is 50,000 a year. Every year.

Read that again. The math is brutal. It is also fixable.

## Step 4: The reality of the true hourly rate

There is one more sanity check that makes the leak impossible to ignore.

Take your gross revenue last year. Divide it by the **total hours you actually worked**, not just the billable ones. Include marketing, admin, sales, networking, all of it.

For a typical solo creative business pulling 150,000 in revenue and working 3,000 total hours a year:

- Apparent rate: 150 per hour (on the proposal).
- Real rate: 50 per hour (revenue divided by total hours worked).

That is the rate that funds your life. Not the one on the proposal.

Most people are quietly shocked when they do this calculation honestly. That shock is the moment pricing finally feels like a survival issue, not a marketing one.

> **Warning:** Do not skip this step because the math feels mean. The number you avoid calculating is the number that is currently running your life.

## Closing the gap. Three levers

Once you see the gap between what you charge and what you need, you have three levers, and only three.

1. **Raise prices.** Usually the fastest. See **01. The Math Behind Raising Your Prices** for the math behind why this is less risky than it feels.
2. **Lower costs.** Slower, but real. Audit recurring software, contractor relationships, office space, tools you do not use.
3. **Increase capacity.** Through delegation, systems, or hiring. We cover this in **03. The Rule of Three and the $1,000-an-Hour Test** and **04. Hiring for Values, Not Just Skills**.

Most fixes are a combination. Rarely just one.

## The 90-day profit recovery plan

Use a simple phased timeline so the work does not collapse into a single overwhelming weekend.

### Month 1: Investigate

Run all four diagnostic steps. Pricing reality. True time. Lifeline number. True hourly rate. Write the findings down in one document. Call it the **case file**. You will reference it for years.

### Month 2: Reconstruct

Update the things downstream of the case file.

- Rate cards.
- Proposal templates.
- Default scope language.
- The way you quote on calls.

Do not announce anything publicly. Just rebuild the artifacts.

### Month 3: Implement

Use the new rates and templates on every new conversation. Old projects keep their old terms. New ones land on the new floor.

Track every reaction. Did anyone walk? Did anyone push back? Did anyone agree without flinching? You are gathering data, not winning arguments.

### Month 4: Review

Compare. Revenue, recovered profit, conversion rate, time per project. Most owners who run this honestly recover **fifteen to thirty percent** of previously lost profit. Without finding new clients. Without working more hours.

That is not a marketing claim. That is what the math returns when you stop ignoring it.

## Use AI to compress the diagnostic

You can run all four steps with paper and a calculator. AI just makes it faster.

A useful prompt:

```text
You are a no-nonsense creative-business CFO. I will give you:

- My current rates per offer
- A list of my real annual costs (incl. salary, taxes, savings, software, tools)
- My target profit in absolute dollars
- My realistic billable hours per year
- My actual total hours worked last year and last year's revenue

Calculate:
1. My lifeline hourly rate
2. My real (revenue / total hours) rate
3. The gap between what I charge and what I need
4. The three changes that would close the biggest portion of the gap, in priority order

Be direct. Do not soften the numbers.
```

Read the output. Stress-test the assumptions. The numbers are only as honest as the inputs you give it.

## Try This

One sitting. Forty-five minutes:

1. Pick one project from the last quarter. Reconstruct every hour spent. Write the total.
2. Divide your last twelve months of revenue by your real total hours worked. Write that rate down.
3. Build your lifeline rate using the formula above. Write it down.
4. Subtract your real rate from your lifeline rate. That is the gap, per hour. Multiply by 1,000 hours to see the annual leak.
5. Pick one of the three levers (raise, lower, or expand). Write the first action you will take this week.

The exercise is uncomfortable. It is also the first time most people see the number that has been quietly running their life.

## Summary

There is a gap between your invoice rate and your true rate. Closing that gap is almost never about more clients. It is about pricing reality, true time tracking, and a clear lifeline number. Once you can see the gap on paper, the path back to healthy margins is a 90-day rebuild of rates, templates, and habits. Most owners recover fifteen to thirty percent of lost profit without changing their workload at all. The work is in the math, not the marketing.
