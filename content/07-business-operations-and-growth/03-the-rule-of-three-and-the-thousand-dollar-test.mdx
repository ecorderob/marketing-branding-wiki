## The trap of the long list

Most creative owners end Sunday with a list of fifteen things they want to do this week. By Friday, they have done five. None of them moved the business much.

The problem is not effort. It is the list. Fifteen priorities is not a list of priorities. It is a wishlist. A real priority list is short, ranked, and ruthless about what does not make the cut.

This page is about two simple filters. The Rule of Three for what you keep. The Thousand-Dollar Test for what you let go.

## The Rule of Three

> **Definition:** The Rule of Three. A prioritization filter where you score every potential activity on Impact and Urgency, then keep only the top three. Everything else goes on a "Not Right Now" list.

The mechanics are simple.

1. List 10 to 20 things you think you should be doing.
2. Score each on Impact, 1 to 5. How much does this move the business?
3. Score each on Urgency, 1 to 5. How time-sensitive is it?
4. Add the two numbers. That is the priority score.
5. Circle the top three. Everything else goes on a list called "Not Right Now."

Three is not arbitrary. It is the largest number of things a normal human can keep moving forward at once without dropping all of them.

**Example.** A solo studio owner brainstorms a list:

| Activity | Impact | Urgency | Score |
|----|----|----|----|
| Complete 3 case studies | 5 | 5 | 10 |
| Raise prices 15% | 4 | 5 | 9 |
| Build a referral system | 4 | 4 | 8 |
| Start an Instagram account | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Redesign the website | 3 | 2 | 5 |
| Email automation | 4 | 2 | 6 |

The top three are obvious. Case studies, prices, referrals. Everything else is real, but it is not for this quarter. The list is honest about that, instead of lying with all fifteen items still in the queue.

> **Tip:** "Not Right Now" is not "Never." Park items there with a date for review. Three months out is usually right. When the date comes, score them again. Some will still be in the bottom half. A few will be obvious top-three items by then.

## Why Three works

People resist the Rule of Three because it feels like giving up. It is not. It is the opposite.

What it does:

- **Prevents shiny-object syndrome.** Most "good ideas" are simply distractions wearing a costume.
- **Forces decisions.** When the top three is full, a new idea has to displace one of them. That is healthy friction.
- **Creates momentum.** A short list of finished projects beats a long list of half-finished ones, every time.

A long task list looks like ambition. A short one is what gets shipped.

## The Thousand-Dollar Test

The Rule of Three tells you what to do. The Thousand-Dollar Test tells you what to stop doing.

> **Definition:** The Thousand-Dollar Test. A simple delegation filter. For any recurring task, ask "Would I pay someone 1,000 dollars an hour to do this?" If the answer is no, that task is not yours to keep.

The filter, applied to common tasks:

- Organizing files. No. Delegate.
- Answering routine support email. No. Delegate.
- Researching software options. No. Delegate or skip.
- Scheduling meetings. No. Delegate or automate.
- Bookkeeping data entry. No. Delegate.
- Strategic client conversation. Yes. Keep.
- Designing a core service offering. Yes. Keep.
- Writing a case study. Yes. Keep.
- Closing a 50,000-dollar deal. Yes. Keep.

The point is not literal. You are not actually paying someone 1,000 an hour. The price tag forces a re-evaluation of what your time is for. Every hour spent on a 25-an-hour task is an hour not spent on a 500-an-hour task.

> **Warning:** "But I like organizing files" is not an answer the test cares about. Hobbies are fine. Hobbies in your billable hours are not.

## What to delegate, in what order

Once you have your "no" list from the Thousand-Dollar Test, you need a sequence. Trying to delegate everything at once usually fails.

A reasonable order:

1. **Admin first.** Scheduling, file organization, expense reports, routine email. Lowest stakes. Easiest handoff. Builds your delegation muscle without much risk.
2. **Routine production support.** Asset management, formatting, exports, simple revisions on templated work.
3. **Communication second-tier.** Standard client check-ins, status updates, project documentation.
4. **Research and operations.** Market research, vendor sourcing, software evaluation.
5. **Specialized creative work.** Eventually. Only after the first four are running smoothly and the Magnet pillar (see **01. Where Your Time Should Actually Go**) is healthy enough to support the cost.

Stage one and two alone often free up ten to fifteen hours a week. That is enough time to triple your Magnet output.

## Document before you delegate

The biggest reason delegation fails is that the owner does not write down what they actually do.

Before handing off any task, spend an hour doing it slowly while recording your screen and narrating each step. Then turn the recording into a one-page document. The document should answer:

- What is the trigger that starts this task?
- What is the expected output?
- What are the three or four most common edge cases?
- Where do I look when something goes wrong?
- What is the success criterion?

This is the single biggest difference between delegation that works and delegation that explodes back into your inbox a month later.

## The Category 3 Time Sink

There is a third filter worth knowing. Some activities masquerade as work but produce nothing.

> **Definition:** A Category 3 Time Sink. An activity that feels like business but does not bring in money or fulfill a paid commitment. Common examples are endless networking, perfectionism past what the client values, watching webinars without applying anything, reorganizing files for the third time, and "staying current" by following every trend.

The diagnostic is one sentence: **Does this activity directly bring in money or fulfill a paid commitment?**

If the answer is no, the activity needs to be minimized, automated, or delegated. Not justified.

This is harder than it sounds. Most Category 3 sinks come with a self-image attached. "I am a serious operator. I read the trade press." "I am a learner. I watch every new course." Those self-images are fine. The activities that go with them mostly belong on the Not Right Now list.

> **Tip:** When complexity creeps into your offerings (the website is hard to navigate, packaging is confusing, clients keep asking what you do), do not redesign anything. Cut. Complexity is a focus problem, not a copy problem. Eliminate offerings until one clear value proposition remains.

## Use AI to triage your list

The Rule of Three benefits from a second opinion before you commit. AI is fast at this.

A useful prompt:

```text
You are a sharp operations advisor for a creative business. I will paste a list of 10 to 20 things I am thinking about doing this quarter. For each one:

- Score Impact 1 to 5 (how much it moves the business)
- Score Urgency 1 to 5 (how time-sensitive)
- Add them for a total

Then:
1. Rank the list highest to lowest
2. Tell me which top three you would pick and why
3. Flag any items that look like Category 3 Time Sinks (work that feels productive but does not bring in money or fulfill a paid commitment)
4. Ask me one challenging question about my list before I commit

Push back. Do not be polite.
```

Treat the output as a sparring partner, not a verdict. The final call is yours.

## Try This

Twenty minutes:

1. Brainstorm 10 to 20 things you think you should be doing this quarter. No filtering yet.
2. Score each on Impact and Urgency. Add the scores.
3. Circle the top three.
4. Take the rest. Apply the Thousand-Dollar Test to each. Mark D for delegate, A for automate, S for skip.
5. Write the top three on a sticky note. Put it where you sit. Do not look at the rest of the list until next quarter's review.

That sticky note is your work. The other list is noise dressed as ambition.

## Summary

A long task list is a wish, not a plan. The Rule of Three forces honest priority by limiting your active list to three top-scoring items. The Thousand-Dollar Test forces honest delegation by separating work that deserves your hours from work that does not. Together, they pull your week back from scattered to focused. The hardest part is not the math. It is letting things drop that you have been carrying out of habit.
